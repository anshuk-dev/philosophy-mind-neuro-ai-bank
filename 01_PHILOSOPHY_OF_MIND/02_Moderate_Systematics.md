### Question 1: Ned Block's China Brain and Functionalist Chauvinism/Liberalism
**Format**: Conceptual Multiple-Choice (Format A)  
**Difficulty Tier**: Moderate / Systematic  
**Primary Thinkers**: Ned Block (1978)

In "Troubles with Functionalism" (1978), Ned Block formulates the celebrated **China Brain** (or Chinese Nation) thought experiment. A billion citizens of China are organized via two-way radios and satellite communications to simulate the exact functional organization, artificial neurons, and transitional states of a human brain. What systemic dilemma does Block argue this poses for functionalism?

- (A) The experiment proves that mental states are strictly supervenient on quantum coherent states inside neuronal microtubules.
- (B) Functionalism is guilty of **chauvinism**—it arbitrarily restricts consciousness only to organic, carbon-based biological organisms.
- (C) The China Brain demonstrates that functional transitions violate the Church-Turing thesis regarding computability.
- (D) Functionalism is guilty of **liberalism**—it ascribes mental states and consciousness to systems that patently lack any qualitative experience (Absent Qualia).

**Correct Answer**: **(D)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
In "Troubles with Functionalism", Ned Block introduced two diagnostic terms to evaluate philosophical theories of mind:
1. *Chauvinism*: A theory is chauvinistic if it withholds mental states from things that actually have them (e.g., Type Identity Theory, which insists pain must be C-fiber excitation, unfairly denying pain to silicon aliens or cephalopods).
2. *Liberalism*: A theory is liberal if it attributes mental states to things that obviously lack them.

Block created the **China Brain** (or "Homunculi-headed system") thought experiment to prove that Functionalism is fatally *liberal*:
Suppose the billion citizens of China simulate the functionally organized neurons of a human brain, controlling an artificial body via radio transmissions. If functionalism is true, this entire collective system must experience genuine qualitative mental states:
> "It has an input and an output... and between them a system of functional transitions. If the body steps on a nail, is there an entity that feels the pain? It seems absurd to suppose that the population of China, acting together as an artificial computer, would be the subject of a toothache or experience the smell of a rose."

Block argues that the system realizes the functional organization without having any conscious qualitative life at all (**Absent Qualia**). Thus, functionalism fails because it mistake structural causal organization for conscious phenomenal experience.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(A) is incorrect**: This is Roger Penrose and Stuart Hameroff’s Orchestrated Objective Reduction (Orch-OR) theory of consciousness (1994), which plays no role in Block’s 1978 critique of functionalism.
- **(B) is incorrect**: Block accuses Type Physicalism of chauvinism, not functionalism; functionalism’s cardinal defect in the China Brain case is *liberalism*.
- **(C) is incorrect**: Block explicitly grants that the China Brain is fully Turing-computable and functionally isomorphic to a human brain; his argument is ontological and phenomenological, not a mathematical challenge to the Church-Turing thesis.

**Primary Textual Reference**: Ned Block, "Troubles with Functionalism", in *Perception and Cognition: Issues in the Foundations of Psychology*, Minnesota Studies in the Philosophy of Science, Vol. 9, ed. C.W. Savage (University of Minnesota Press, 1978), pp. 261–325.

### Question 2: Physicalist Responses to Jackson: The Ability Hypothesis
**Format**: Conceptual Multiple-Choice (Format A)  
**Difficulty Tier**: Moderate / Systematic  
**Primary Thinkers**: Laurence Nemirow (1980), David Lewis (1988, 1990)

In response to Frank Jackson’s Knowledge Argument, David Lewis and Laurence Nemirow formulated the **Ability Hypothesis**. How does the Ability Hypothesis preserve physicalism without denying that Mary undergoes a genuine psychological change upon seeing red for the first time?

- (A) It claims that Mary’s pre-release education was simply incomplete because physicalism cannot account for quantum entanglement in visual pigments.
- (B) It argues that upon release, Mary gains **practical abilities / know-how** (such as the ability to recognize, imagine, and remember red), not new **propositional knowledge / know-that** about non-physical facts.
- (C) It argues that Mary gains a new non-physical epiphenomenal quale that supervenes on an entirely new neural firing pattern in cortical area V4.
- (D) It asserts that Mary already had the perceptual experience of red in black-and-white because shades of gray contain dormant wavelengths of the visible spectrum.

**Correct Answer**: **(B)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
The Ability Hypothesis, formulated by Laurence Nemirow and extensively defended by David Lewis ("What Experience Teaches", 1988), challenges the second premise of Jackson’s syllogism: "Upon release, Mary gains new knowledge of a fact."

Lewis draws on Gilbert Ryle's classic distinction between:
- *Knowledge-that* (propositional knowledge: facts that can be stated in true assertions).
- *Knowledge-how* (practical abilities, skills, and capacities: e.g., knowing how to ride a bicycle or play the piano).

Lewis argues that in her black-and-white room, Mary possessed *all propositional knowledge* concerning colour vision. What happens when she steps outside and sees a ripe tomato? She does not discover a new non-physical truth about the universe. Rather, she acquires a set of **practical cognitive skills**:
1. The ability to *remember* the experience of red.
2. The ability to *imagine* red at will.
3. The ability to *recognize* red objects in the future without analytical deduction.

Because acquiring an ability or practical skill is not acquiring knowledge of a new non-physical fact, physicalism remains completely intact: all facts remain physical facts.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(A) is incorrect**: Physicalists grant Jackson's premise that Mary's physical knowledge was complete; they attack the inference that what she gains upon release is propositional knowledge.
- **(C) is incorrect**: Admitting that Mary gains a "non-physical quale" would be conceding the entire argument to Jackson's property dualism; the Ability Hypothesis strictly denies that any non-physical quale exists.
- **(D) is incorrect**: This is an absurd empirical claim; black-and-white light does not contain red wavelengths, and physicalists do not dispute the novelty of Mary's sensory experience.

**Primary Textual Reference**: David Lewis, "What Experience Teaches", *Proceedings of the Russellian Society* (1988); reprinted in *Mind and Cognition*, ed. W.G. Lycan (Blackwell, 1990), pp. 499–519; Laurence Nemirow, "Review of Thomas Nagel's *Mortal Questions*", *Philosophical Review* 89(3) (1980): 473–477.

### Question 3: Thomas Nagel and the Subjective Character of Experience
**Format**: Assertion-Reason (Format B)  
**Difficulty Tier**: Moderate / Systematic  
**Primary Thinkers**: Thomas Nagel (1974)

Given below are two statements: one is labelled as **Assertion (A)** and the other is labelled as **Reason (R)**:

**Assertion (A)**: In "What is it like to be a bat?" (1974), Thomas Nagel argues that no amount of physical, neurophysiological, or functional information about a bat’s echolocation system can allow a human being to know *what it is like for a bat to be a bat*.  
**Reason (R)**: Scientific physical descriptions are designed to achieve objective, observer-independent descriptions by moving away from any particular point of view, whereas every conscious phenomenal experience is inextricably tethered to a specific, subjective point of view.

In light of the above statements, choose the correct answer:
- (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the strictly correct philosophical explanation of (A).
- (B) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
- (C) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
- (D) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A).

**Correct Answer**: **(A)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
Nagel’s landmark 1974 paper locates the fundamental crisis of physicalism not in technical gaps in neuroscience, but in the irreconcilable epistemological mismatch between physical objectivity and phenomenal subjectivity.

Nagel observes:
> "Conscious experience is a widespread phenomenon... fundamentally an organism has conscious mental states if and only if there is something that it is like to *be* that organism—something it is like *for* the organism."

In **Assertion (A)**, Nagel chooses bats because they are mammals (phylogenetically related to us) that possess a sensory apparatus radically alien to ours: echolocation / sonar. Even if a human neuroscientist maps every single acoustic nerve fiber, sonic reflection, and brain region in the bat, the human can at best imagine what it would be like *for a human to behave as a bat*, not what it is like *for the bat itself*.

In **Reason (R)**, Nagel reveals the deep conceptual reason: The entire progress of physical science consists in reducing subjectivity by abstracting away from human sensory vantage points toward objective, observer-neutral formulations (e.g., understanding lightning not as visual flash, but as electrical discharge). However, when applying this method to consciousness itself, the method collapses:
> "If the subjective character of experience is fully comprehensible only from one point of view, then any shift to greater objectivity—that is, less attachment to a specific viewpoint—does not take us closer to the real nature of the phenomenon: it takes us farther away from it."

Thus, (R) provides the definitive theoretical rationale for (A).

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(B) is incorrect**: (R) is an exact paraphrase of Nagel's argument concerning the directional movement of scientific objectivity vs. phenomenal subjectivity.
- **(C) is incorrect**: (A) is one of the most famous formulations in contemporary epistemology and philosophy of mind.
- **(D) is incorrect**: (R) is not merely a supplementary point; it is the structural core of Nagel’s entire essay, explaining why physicalism cannot assimilate phenomenal consciousness using its standard reductive toolkit.

**Primary Textual Reference**: Thomas Nagel, "What is it like to be a bat?", *Philosophical Review* 83(4) (1974): 435–450.

### Question 4: The Chinese Room and Searle's Critique of Computationalism
**Format**: Conceptual Multiple-Choice (Format A)  
**Difficulty Tier**: Moderate / Systematic  
**Primary Thinkers**: John Searle (1980)

In "Minds, Brains, and Programs" (1980), John Searle argues against "Strong AI" using the Chinese Room thought experiment. When critics proposed the **Systems Reply**—asserting that while the man locked in the room does not understand Chinese, the *whole system* (consisting of the man, the rule book, the paper slips, and the room) does understand Chinese—how did Searle refute this reply?

- (A) He showed that the Chinese room produces output too slowly to participate in real-time conversational linguistics.
- (B) He instructed the man to memorize the rule book, internalize the symbol manipulations, and calculate everything in his head; even then, the man understands no Chinese, and since the whole system is now contained inside the man, the system understands no Chinese either.
- (C) He argued that the paper slips have no mass, and therefore cannot transmit causal force to the physical environment.
- (D) He demonstrated that English grammar is inherently context-free, whereas Chinese grammar is context-sensitive and cannot be computed by a Turing machine.

**Correct Answer**: **(B)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
The **Systems Reply** (originally formulated by Ned Block, Jerry Fodor, and Berkeley cognitive scientists) concedes that the clerk inside the room does not understand Chinese, but argues that understanding is an emergent property of the larger computational system as an integrated whole:
> "Understanding is not being ascribed to the mere person; it is being ascribed to this whole system of which he is a part."

Searle decisively dismantled the Systems Reply in his 1980 paper by absorbing the entire system into the individual:
> "My response to the systems reply is quite simple: let the individual internalize all of these elements of the system. He memorizes the rules in the ledger and the banks of Chinese symbols and he does all the calculations in his head. The individual then incorporates the entire system; there isn’t any part of the system that he does not encompass. We still cannot say that he understands Chinese, since he doesn't know what any of the symbols mean... If he doesn't understand Chinese on that basis, there is no way the system could, because the system is now a part of him."

Searle's core thesis remains intact: syntax (formal symbol manipulation) is neither constitutive of nor sufficient for semantics (intrinsic intentionality and mental meaning).

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(A) is incorrect**: Computational processing speed is a purely technological contingency; Searle's objection is conceptual and metaphysical.
- **(C) is incorrect**: Searle’s argument is purely semantic and intentional; he never makes an absurd physics claim about paper slips having no mass.
- **(D) is incorrect**: Searle explicitly grants that the program passes the Turing Test perfectly and that its Chinese linguistic output is indistinguishable from a native speaker; his point is that simulation is not duplication.

**Primary Textual Reference**: John Searle, "Minds, Brains, and Programs", *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 3(3) (1980): 417–457.

### Question 5: Sydney Shoemaker and the Inverted Spectrum Thought Experiment
**Format**: Conceptual Multiple-Choice (Format A)  
**Difficulty Tier**: Moderate / Systematic  
**Primary Thinkers**: Sydney Shoemaker (1982), John Locke (*Essay*, 1690), Ned Block (1990)

The **Inverted Spectrum Argument** (traced back to John Locke and modernized by Sydney Shoemaker and Ned Block) imagines two individuals, **Tom and Jerry**, whose color experiences are systematically inverted relative to one another (whenever Tom experiences the qualitative sensation of *red*, Jerry experiences the qualitative sensation of *green*, and vice versa). If Tom and Jerry share the exact same behavioral functional organization (both call ripe strawberries "red", both stop at "red" traffic lights, both describe blood as warm-colored), what does this thought experiment systematically prove against Machine Functionalism?

- (A) Tom and Jerry are visually colorblind and should have their driving licenses revoked by municipal traffic authorities.
- (B) Functionalism defines mental states exclusively in terms of their causal relations to inputs, behavioral outputs, and other mental states; if Tom and Jerry have identical functional roles yet possess radically inverted phenomenal qualia, then phenomenal qualia cannot be identical to or captured by functional roles, demonstrating that functionalism leaves out the subjective character of experience (*qualia liberalism*).
- (C) The inverted spectrum proves that colors do not exist as physical electromagnetic wavelengths in optics.
- (D) The argument demonstrates that Jerry's brain is an immaterial Cartesian soul while Tom's brain is made of biological silicon.

**Correct Answer**: **(B)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
The Inverted Spectrum Argument exposes the vulnerability of **Functionalism**:
1. **The Functionalist Definition**:
   - According to functionalism (Putnam, Armstrong, Lewis), a mental state type is defined entirely by its causal/functional role: its relations to sensory inputs, motor outputs, and other internal states.
   - For a functionalist, the state of "seeing red" is just that state that is typically caused by red objects (wavelengths ~700 nm), which causes the belief "that is red", and causes utterances like "that rose is red".
2. **The Intrapersonal and Interpersonal Inversion**:
   - Imagine Tom and Jerry look at a ripe fire engine.
   - Both receive 700 nm photon inputs.
   - Both utter: *"Look at that bright red fire engine!"*
   - Both react identically in every possible cognitive, behavioral, verbal, and computational test.
   - Yet internally, what it is like for Tom is qualitative sensation $Q_{\text{red}}$, while for Jerry it is qualitative sensation $Q_{\text{green}}$.
3. **The Consequence for Functionalism**:
   - If their functional roles are **identical**, functionalism must claim they are in the **exact same mental state**.
   - But their phenomenal mental states are **different** ($Q_{\text{red}} \ne Q_{\text{green}}$).
   - Therefore, phenomenal mental states (qualia) are not functional states! Functionalism fails to account for the qualitative "what-it-is-likeness" of conscious states.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(A) is incorrect**: Colorblindness (dichromacy) involves an inability to discriminate colors; Tom and Jerry have full trichromatic color discrimination with identical behavioral competence.
- **(C) is incorrect**: The thought experiment takes physical optics for granted; the dispute is purely about the relationship between physical wavelengths, functional wiring, and subjective qualia.
- **(D) is incorrect**: Both Tom and Jerry are physical human beings with identical functional neural architectures; no metaphysical dualism is required to formulate the argument.

**Primary Textual Reference**: Sydney Shoemaker, "The Inverted Spectrum", *Journal of Philosophy* 79, no. 7 (1982): 357–381; John Locke, *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1690), Book II, Chapter 32, Section 15; Ned Block, "Inverted Earth", *Philosophical Perspectives* 4 (1990): 53–79.

### Question 6: Donald Davidson's Anomalous Monism and the Absence of Psychophysical Laws
**Format**: Conceptual Multiple-Choice (Format A)  
**Difficulty Tier**: Moderate / Systematic  
**Primary Thinkers**: Donald Davidson ("Mental Events", 1970)

In "Mental Events" (1970), Donald Davidson formulated **Anomalous Monism**, reconciling the causal efficacy of the mental with the physicalist rejection of Cartesian dualism. Davidson's system rests upon three seemingly contradictory principles:
1. *The Principle of Causal Interaction*: At least some mental events interact causally with physical events.
2. *The Principle of the Nomological Character of Causality*: Events related as cause and effect must fall under strict deterministic laws.
3. *The Anomalism of the Mental*: There are no strict deterministic laws on the basis of which mental events can predict, explain, or be explained by other events (no strict psychophysical or psychological laws).

How does Davidson resolve this apparent contradiction to establish **Token Identity** without type reduction?

- (A) Every individual mental event is token-identical to a physical event ($m = p$); causal relations hold between concrete individual events regardless of how they are described; when two events are causally related, there is a strict law that subsumes them under their **physical descriptions**, even though no strict laws can ever connect mental descriptions with physical descriptions due to the normative constitutive ideal of rationality governing mental vocabulary.
- (B) Davidson argues that mental events are made of ectoplasm that occasionally breaks through physical reality via quantum anomalies.
- (C) He resolves the contradiction by proving that physical laws are illusions invented by physicists to secure university research grants.
- (D) Davidson claims that mental events never cause anything and that human actions are complete hallucinations.

**Correct Answer**: **(A)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
Davidson’s Anomalous Monism is one of the most brilliant solutions to the mind-body problem in 20th-century analytic philosophy:
1. **The Trilemma**:
   - If mental events cause physical events (Principle 1), and causation requires strict deterministic laws (Principle 2), it seems there must be strict psychophysical laws ($\text{Mental State } M \to \text{Physical State } P$).
   - But Principle 3 denies that such laws exist! How can all three be true?
2. **Davidson's Dual-Vocabulary Ontology**:
   - Davidson distinguishes between **events themselves** (concrete particulars in spacetime) and **descriptions of events**.
   - Causation is an extensional relation between events: event $c$ causes event $e$. This holds no matter how we describe them.
   - Laws are intensional/linguistic: an event is subsumed under a law only under a specific vocabulary or description.
3. **The Constitutive Ideal of Rationality**:
   - Physical vocabulary is governed by constitutive principles of measurement, spatio-temporal continuity, and physical conservation laws.
   - Mental vocabulary (beliefs, desires, intentions) is governed by the **Constitutive Ideal of Rationality** (coherence, consistency, norms of rationality, and the Principle of Charity).
   - Because the two domains operate under incommensurable constitutive criteria, there can be **no bridge laws** linking mental predicates to physical predicates (no type-type identity).
4. **The Monist Synthesis (Token Identity)**:
   - When my desire to drink causes my arm to reach, that specific mental event *is* a specific physical event (token identity).
   - Under its physical description ("neuronal spike train in motor cortex"), it is subsumed under a strict physical law.
   - Under its mental description ("desire for water"), it is anomalous (free from strict deterministic laws). Thus: **Monism** (all events are physical), but **Anomalous** (mental types cannot be reduced to physical types).

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(B) is incorrect**: Davidson is an uncompromising physicalist monist; he rejects Cartesian ectoplasm, vitalism, and dualism completely.
- **(C) is incorrect**: Physical laws are strictly real and deterministic in Davidson's framework (Principle of the Nomological Character of Causality).
- **(D) is incorrect**: Principle 1 explicitly affirms that mental events are causally efficacious in producing physical bodily actions.

**Primary Textual Reference**: Donald Davidson, "Mental Events", in *Experience and Theory*, ed. L. Foster and J. W. Swanson (University of Massachusetts Press, 1970), reprinted in *Essays on Actions and Events* (Oxford University Press, 1980), pp. 207–227.

### Question 7: Jerry Fodor and the Language of Thought: Mentalese and Intentional Realism
**Format**: Conceptual Multiple-Choice (Format A)  
**Difficulty Tier**: Moderate / Systematic  
**Primary Thinkers**: Jerry A. Fodor (*The Language of Thought*, 1975; *Psychosemantics*, 1987)

In *The Language of Thought* (1975), Jerry Fodor defended **Intentional Realism**—the thesis that propositional attitudes (beliefs, desires) are literally real, causally efficacious internal states—by introducing the hypothesis of a **Language of Thought (LOT)**, colloquially called **"Mentalese"**. What are the two core cognitive phenomena—**Productivity** and **Systematicity**—that Fodor argues can only be explained if cognitive representations possess a compositional linguistic syntax?

- (A) Human thought is **productive** because an infinite number of distinct thoughts can be generated from a finite vocabulary of mental symbols, and **systematic** because the cognitive capacity to think certain thoughts (e.g., "John loves Mary") is intrinsically and nomologically connected to the capacity to think structurally related thoughts ("Mary loves John"), which requires that mental representations possess combinatorial syntactic structure.
- (B) Fodor argues that thoughts are non-compositional, continuous analog fluid waves that cannot be divided into discrete symbolic parts.
- (C) Mentalese is a spoken natural language dialect discovered in ancient Mesopotamian archaeological tablets.
- (D) Productivity means human brains produce economic manufactured goods, while Systematicity means human beings follow corporate bureaucratic spreadsheets.

**Correct Answer**: **(A)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
Jerry Fodor’s Computational Theory of Mind (CTM) argues that thinking is computational symbol manipulation over an internal, mental representational system:
1. **The Nature of Mentalese**:
   - Mentalese is not English, Hindi, or Chinese; it is the innate, universal formal language of cognition.
   - Mental states (like believing that it is raining) are relations between a person and a mental sentence tokens in the head: $\text{Belief}(\text{Agent}, \lceil \text{Rain}(t) \rceil)$.
2. **The Argument from Productivity**:
   - Human competence is unbounded: you can understand and contemplate thoughts you have never heard or conceived before (e.g., *"A purple zebra is playing the flute on Saturn's rings"*).
   - Just like natural languages (Chomsky), finite minds can only achieve infinite expressive capacity if representations are built **compositionally** from a finite set of discrete symbols combined via recursive syntactic rules.
3. **The Argument from Systematicity**:
   - You never meet a human who can understand *"John loves Mary"* but lacks the cognitive architecture to understand *"Mary loves John"*.
   - The ability to entertain one thought systematically entails the ability to entertain permutations of its constituents.
   - If mental representations were uncomposed, holistic pictures or non-symbolic connectionist weight vectors, systematicity would be an inexplicable miracle. Systematicity is only guaranteed if representations have constituent parts (syntax) that can be recombined according to formal rules.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(B) is incorrect**: Continuous analog fluid waves are what connectionists and dynamical systems theorists proposed, which Fodor vehemently attacked for failing to explain productivity and systematicity.
- **(C) is incorrect**: Mentalese is an innate cognitive computational vehicle in the brain, not a historical spoken dialect or ancient tablet script.
- **(D) is incorrect**: Fodor’s terms are technical concepts in mathematical linguistics and cognitive epistemology, completely unrelated to industrial economics or office bureaucracy.

**Primary Textual Reference**: Jerry A. Fodor, *The Language of Thought* (Harvard University Press, 1975); Jerry A. Fodor, *Psychosemantics: The Problem of Meaning in the Philosophy of Mind* (MIT Press, 1987), Chapter 1.

### Question 8: Tyler Burge's Anti-Individualism: Semantic Externalism and the "Arthritis" Case
**Format**: Conceptual Multiple-Choice (Format A)  
**Difficulty Tier**: Moderate / Systematic  
**Primary Thinkers**: Tyler Burge ("Individualism and the Mental", 1979), Hilary Putnam (1975)

In his landmark paper "Individualism and the Mental" (1979), Tyler Burge extended Putnam's semantic externalism (*"meanings just ain't in the head"*) from physical natural kinds to the very content of human beliefs and mental states. In Burge's famous thought experiment involving **Bert and his belief about "arthritis"**, what does Burge prove regarding **Anti-Individualism (Social Externalism)**?

- (A) The thought experiment proves that doctors always misdiagnose joint pain due to hospital billing incentives.
- (B) Bert erroneously believes that he has "arthritis in his thigh"; in an alternative counterfactual linguistic community where the word "thigh-inflammation" is standardly included in the medical term "thirtritis", an exact physical and neurophysiological duplicate of Bert would hold a different belief; therefore, the propositional contents of a person's mental states are partly individuated by their social-linguistic environment, not solely by their internal neurobiology.
- (C) Burge proves that words have no meaning and that human communication is impossible.
- (D) Bert suffers from rheumatoid arthritis in his knee, proving that physical inflammation causes Cartesian substance dualism.

**Correct Answer**: **(B)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
Burge's target is **Individualism** (the classical Cartesian/Internalist doctrine that an agent's mental states are entirely determined by what lies within their physical body and skull):
1. **The Actual World Scenario**:
   - Bert has a pain in his thigh. He goes to his doctor and says: *"I have arthritis in my thigh."*
   - In our actual English-speaking world, "arthritis" is defined medically strictly as an inflammation of the **joints**.
   - Bert has a false belief: he believes that *arthritis* has spread to his thigh. His mental state contains the concept **arthritis**, which he partially misunderstands.
2. **The Counterfactual Scenario**:
   - Imagine a counterfactual world where Bert’s physical history, internal brain states, non-intentional behaviors, and bodily atoms are **molecule-for-molecule identical** to the actual Bert.
   - However, in this counterfactual society, the medical community uses the word "thirtritis" (pronounced or spelled identically) to cover *both* joint inflammation and muscle/thigh inflammation.
   - When counterfactual Bert says *"I have thirtritis in my thigh"*, his belief is **true**! And his belief does not contain the concept of arthritis (since arthritis as defined in our world does not exist in their language).
3. **The Philosophical Verdict**:
   - Actual Bert and Counterfactual Bert are **physically, neurophysiologically, and internally identical**.
   - Yet they have **different beliefs** with different propositional contents!
   - Therefore, what belief an agent has is not determined solely by internal physical brain states, but is constitutively dependent upon the **social-linguistic practices of the wider linguistic community**. Mentality is social and externalist.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(A) is incorrect**: Medical billing is a modern healthcare administration issue, irrelevant to the philosophical debate on semantic externalism and belief individuation.
- **(C) is incorrect**: Burge is an intentional realist; he proves how linguistic conventions successfully individuate objective semantic content.
- **(D) is incorrect**: The thought experiment takes medical pathology as a background scenario; it does not defend Cartesian dualism or medical etiology.

**Primary Textual Reference**: Tyler Burge, "Individualism and the Mental", *Midwest Studies in Philosophy* 4, no. 1 (1979): 73–121; Tyler Burge, *Foundations of Mind* (Oxford University Press, 2007).

### Question 9: Colin McGinn and New Mysterianism: Cognitive Closure and the Mind-Body Nexus
**Format**: Conceptual Multiple-Choice (Format A)  
**Difficulty Tier**: Moderate / Systematic  
**Primary Thinkers**: Colin McGinn ("Can We Solve the Mind-Body Problem?", 1989), Noam Chomsky (1975)

In "Can We Solve the Mind-Body Problem?" (*Mind*, 1989), Colin McGinn formulated **New Mysterianism**, arguing that while physicalism is true in nature, the mind-body problem is permanently insoluble for human beings. What is McGinn's concept of **"Cognitive Closure"**, and why does he argue that humans can never discover the bridge property ($\mathcal{P}$) that connects brain states to conscious experience?

- (A) Just as a chimpanzee’s mind is cognitively closed to quantum electrodynamics due to evolutionary biological limits, human minds are cognitively closed to the psychophysical link property $\mathcal{P}$ because our cognitive faculties are constrained to two mutually isolated, non-overlapping channels—introspective phenomenal awareness and spatial-perceptual empirical science—neither of which can ever access the property that links physical space to phenomenal qualia.
- (B) Cognitive closure means human brains physically stop functioning at the moment of death.
- (C) Cognitive closure is an engineering defect in commercial brain-computer interface headsets.
- (D) McGinn argues that consciousness is a supernatural miracle that violates all laws of mathematics.

**Correct Answer**: **(A)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
McGinn's New Mysterianism offers a naturalistic, non-magical explanation for why the mind-body problem has resisted centuries of brilliant philosophy:
1. **The Existence of a Natural Solution**:
   - McGinn is a naturalist: there is nothing supernatural or miraculous about consciousness. There is some natural, objective physical-ontological property $\mathcal{P}$ of the brain that accounts for how conscious states arise from physical tissue.
2. **The Principle of Cognitive Closure (Chomsky/McGinn)**:
   - A type of mind $\mathcal{M}$ is **cognitively closed** with respect to a property or theory $\mathcal{T}$ if the concept-forming procedures of $\mathcal{M}$ cannot in principle grasp $\mathcal{T}$.
   - A monkey can never grasp the Riemann Hypothesis or general relativity; this is not an embarrassment for physics, but a natural fact of primate evolutionary biology.
3. **The Epistemic Dilemma of the Human Mind**:
   - Humans have only two avenues to investigate reality:
     1. *Introspection*: Gives us direct access to phenomenal conscious states (pain, color, thought), but introspection gives us **zero information about physical brain tissue, neurons, or spatial properties**.
     2. *Perception & Physical Science*: Gives us access to the physical world, but physical perception is **strictly spatial** (shapes, sizes, electrical potentials, neurochemical synapses).
   - The mystery property $\mathcal{P}$ must bridge spatial brain states with non-spatial phenomenal qualia.
   - But we cannot reach $\mathcal{P}$ from introspection (it reveals no brain properties), and we cannot reach $\mathcal{P}$ from spatial science (looking inside the skull reveals only spatial arrangements of matter, never qualia).
   - Therefore, the human cognitive architecture is structurally blind to $\mathcal{P}$. The mind-body problem is a mystery to us, not because it is magic, but because human brains did not evolve the sensory or conceptual equipment to understand their own generation of consciousness.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(B) is incorrect**: Biological mortality is death; McGinn's cognitive closure is an epistemological concept about the boundary limits of human intelligibility.
- **(C) is incorrect**: Conflates an epistemological thesis with electrical hardware limitations in neuro-technology headsets.
- **(D) is incorrect**: McGinn explicitly insists on naturalism and rejects supernatural miracles; the link is physical and natural, but human intelligence lacks the cognitive capacity to grasp it.

**Primary Textual Reference**: Colin McGinn, "Can We Solve the Mind-Body Problem?", *Mind* 98, no. 391 (1989): 349–366; Colin McGinn, *The Problem of Consciousness* (Blackwell, 1991).

### Question 10: Daniel Dennett's Intentional Stance: The Hierarchy of Predictive Strategies
**Format**: Conceptual Multiple-Choice (Format A)  
**Difficulty Tier**: Moderate / Systematic  
**Primary Thinkers**: Daniel C. Dennett (*The Intentional Stance*, 1987)

In *The Intentional Stance* (1987), Daniel Dennett developed an instrumentalist / pragmatist philosophy of mind that explains how we attribute beliefs, desires, and rationality to systems. Dennett outlines three distinct predictive strategies or "stances". What is the foundational hierarchy of the **Physical Stance**, the **Design Stance**, and the **Intentional Stance**?

- (A) The **Physical Stance** predicts behavior using basic physical laws (mass, gravity, chemistry); the **Design Stance** bypasses physical micro-details and predicts behavior assuming the system will perform according to its designed function; and the **Intentional Stance** predicts behavior by treating the system as a rational agent with beliefs and desires appropriate to its environmental niche, acting to satisfy its goals.
- (B) The Physical Stance is for sports athletes; the Design Stance is for fashion architects; and the Intentional Stance is for criminal prosecutors.
- (C) The three stances represent stages in childhood psychoanalysis developed by Sigmund Freud.
- (D) Dennett argues that the Intentional Stance is only applicable to human beings who possess Cartesian immaterial souls.

**Correct Answer**: **(A)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
Dennett's framework dissolves the metaphysical dilemma of intentionality by showing that intentionality is a **predictive stance** that humans adopt toward complex systems:
1. **The Physical Stance**:
   - The lowest and most fundamental level: we predict an object's behavior using the laws of physics, chemistry, and subatomic mechanics.
   - Example: predicting how a rock falls when dropped, or predicting an electronic circuit by calculating transistor voltages and copper resistance.
   - *Limitation*: For complex systems (like a computer playing chess or a human navigating a city), predicting behavior via quantum mechanics or calculating trillions of molecular collisions is computationally impossible.
2. **The Design Stance**:
   - A higher-level abstraction: we predict behavior based on the assumption that the system will behave according to its design or biological function, ignoring underlying physical material.
   - Example: predicting that an alarm clock will ring at 7:00 AM, or predicting that stepping on the brake pedal of a car will stop the vehicle. We don't care whether the clock is mechanical gears or digital chips; we predict based on function.
3. **The Intentional Stance**:
   - The highest-level, most pragmatic strategy: used for complex goal-directed systems (chess computers, animals, humans, corporations).
   - How it works:
     1. Treat the system as a **rational agent**.
     2. Attribute to it the beliefs it ought to have given its perceptual capabilities and environment.
     3. Attribute to it the desires it ought to have given its biological/functional needs.
     4. Predict that it will choose the action that rationally achieves its desires in light of its beliefs.
   - Example: In chess, you predict that the computer will move its queen to defend its king, not by calculating physical silicon voltages or inspecting code loops, but by asking: *"What is the rational move for a player who wants to protect their king?"*
   - Dennett's Pragmatic Realism: If the intentional stance works reliably and yields true predictive fruitfulness, then the system **genuinely has beliefs and desires**; beliefs are real patterns in behavior, not mystical internal Cartesian states.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(B) is incorrect**: Uses colloquial colloquialisms (sports, fashion, courtrooms) that completely misrepresent Dennett's cognitive science methodology.
- **(C) is incorrect**: Conflates Dennett's predictive stances with Freudian psychoanalytic developmental stages (Oral, Anal, Phallic).
- **(D) is incorrect**: Dennett is a renowned physicalist who vehemently denies Cartesian souls; he applies the intentional stance to chess computers, thermostats, animals, and humans alike.

**Primary Textual Reference**: Daniel C. Dennett, *The Intentional Stance* (MIT Press / Bradford Books, 1987), Chapters 1 and 2; Daniel C. Dennett, "True Believers: The Intentional Strategy and Why It Works", in *Scientific Explanation*, ed. A. F. Heath (Oxford University Press, 1981).

### Question 11: Donald Davidson's Anomalous Monism and the Causal Paradox of the Mental
**Format**: Conceptual / Dialectical (Format C)  
**Difficulty Tier**: Moderate / Systematic  
**Primary Thinkers**: Donald Davidson ("Mental Events", 1970)

In his seminal paper *"Mental Events"* (1970), Donald Davidson presents three premises that appear to form an inconsistent triad:
1. **The Principle of Causal Interaction**: At least some mental events interact causally with physical events.
2. **The Principle of the Nomological Character of Causality**: Where there is causality, there must be a strict, exceptionless, deterministic law (*strict nomological law*).
3. **The Anomalism of the Mental**: There are no strict deterministic laws on the basis of which mental events can be predicted and explained.

How does Davidson's theory of **Anomalous Monism** resolve this apparent contradiction?

- (A) By denying that mental events interact with the physical world, embracing pure epiphenomenalism.
- (B) By demonstrating that modern quantum physics contains no strict deterministic laws, thereby dissolving the second premise.
- (C) By distinguishing between *events* as concrete individuals and *descriptions* of events: every individual mental event is token-identical to a physical event (*Token Identity*), but events instantiate strict causal laws only when picked out under their physical descriptions, not under their mental descriptions, because the mental vocabulary is governed by constitutive principles of rationality and coherence.
- (D) By asserting that type-identity holds, meaning every mental property corresponds one-to-one with a unique neurochemical property.

**Correct Answer**: **(C)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
Donald Davidson's **Anomalous Monism** (1970) is one of the most sophisticated non-reductive physicalist positions in analytic philosophy:
1. **The Apparent Contradiction**:
   - If mental events cause physical events (Principle 1), and causation requires strict laws (Principle 2), it seems mental events must fall under strict laws. Yet Davidson defends the Anomalism of the Mental (Principle 3).
2. **The Resolution**:
   - Davidson insists that causality is an extensional relation between **concrete token events** in spacetime. If event $m$ causes event $p$, that causal link holds regardless of how the events are described.
   - However, **laws are linguistic/intensional**: an event falls under a law only when described in a particular vocabulary.
   - When event $e$ is described in physical terms (e.g., neural firings, voltages), it falls under a strict, closed physical law.
   - When that same token event $e$ is described in mental terms (e.g., belief, desire, intention), it cannot fall under strict laws because the attribution of mental states is governed by the **constitutive principle of rationality** (the Principle of Charity).
3. **Conclusion**:
   - Metaphysically, every mental event is identical to a physical event (hence **Monism**).
   - Conceptually, psychological predicates cannot be translated or reduced via bridge laws to physical predicates (hence **Anomalous**).

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(A) is incorrect**: Davidson takes mental causation as his first non-negotiable premise; he does not reject causal interaction.
- **(B) is incorrect**: While quantum physics is probabilistic, Davidson's argument relies on the conceptual disparity between the normative space of reasons and the descriptive space of physics, not on quantum mechanics.
- **(D) is incorrect**: Davidson explicitly rejects Type Identity Physicalism; Anomalous Monism entails *Token Identity* without *Type Identity*.

**Primary Textual Reference**: Donald Davidson, "Mental Events", in *Experience and Theory*, ed. L. Foster and J. W. Swanson (University of Massachusetts Press, 1970); reprinted in *Essays on Actions and Events* (Oxford University Press, 1980), pp. 207–225.

### Question 12: Saul Kripke's Modal Argument against Type Physicalism
**Format**: Modal Logic & Analytic Argument (Format C)  
**Difficulty Tier**: Moderate / Systematic  
**Primary Thinkers**: Saul Kripke (*Naming and Necessity*, 1980, Lecture III)

In Lecture III of *Naming and Necessity* (1980), Saul Kripke formulates a famous modal argument against the Type Identity Theory (e.g., the claim that *"Pain is C-fiber stimulation"*). Why cannot the physicalist explain away the apparent contingency of "Pain is C-fiber stimulation" in the same way science explains away the apparent contingency of "Heat is molecular motion"?

- (A) Because Kripke proved that physical fibers do not exist in any possible world where human language is spoken.
- (B) Because "pain" is a non-rigid designator that changes its reference depending on whether an agent is asleep or awake.
- (C) Because identity statements in empirical science are always logically contingent and can be refuted by a single counterexample.
- (D) Because both "pain" and "C-fiber stimulation" are **rigid designators** picked out by their essential properties; while heat is an objective phenomenon picked out by an accidental epistemic appearance (the sensation of heat, which could have been caused by something else), pain is picked out by the very sensation of pain itself, meaning the appearance of pain is identical to the reality of pain ($A \equiv 	ext{Pain}$), leaving no room for a contingent wedge between the feeling and the phenomenon.

**Correct Answer**: **(D)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
Saul Kripke's modal refutation of Identity Physicalism in *Naming and Necessity* rests on his theory of **Rigid Designators**:
1. **The Necessity of Identity**:
   - If $X$ and $Y$ are rigid designators (designating the same object in all possible worlds where that object exists), then if $X = Y$ is true, it is **necessarily true** ($X = Y \implies \Box(X = Y)$).
2. **The Scientific Paradigm (Heat = Molecular Motion)**:
   - "Heat = Molecular Motion" is a necessary a posteriori truth. Why did it seem contingent to us?
   - Because we initially identified "heat" by its contingent epistemic intermediary: *the sensation it produces in human skin*. We can conceive of a world where creatures feel the sensation of heat from something other than molecular motion, or where molecular motion does not cause that sensation.
3. **The Asymmetry with Pain**:
   - Can we do the same for "Pain = C-fiber stimulation"? **No.**
   - Pain is not picked out by a contingent intermediary sensation; pain is picked out by *the feeling of pain itself*.
   - To be in pain *is* to feel pain. There is no appearance/reality distinction for pain: if it feels like pain, it is pain.
   - Since we can clearly conceive of C-fiber stimulation occurring without any feeling of pain (a philosophical zombie), and we can conceive of pain occurring without C-fibers (multiple realizability), and both terms are rigid designators, the identity statement cannot be necessarily true. And if it is not necessarily true, it cannot be true at all!

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(A) is incorrect**: Kripke does not deny the physical existence of C-fibers; he denies the metaphysical identity between mental qualia and physical fibers.
- **(B) is incorrect**: Kripke's central thesis is precisely that "pain" *is* a rigid designator, not a non-rigid description.
- **(C) is incorrect**: Kripke proved that theoretical identity statements between rigid designators (like "Water is $H_2O$") are *necessary a posteriori*, not contingent.

**Primary Textual Reference**: Saul A. Kripke, *Naming and Necessity* (Harvard University Press, 1980), Lecture III, pp. 144–155; Christopher Hill, *Sensations: A Defense of Type Materialism* (Cambridge University Press, 1991).

### Question 13: Tyler Burge's Anti-Individualism and Social Externalism
**Format**: Thought Experiment Analysis (Format B)  
**Difficulty Tier**: Moderate / Systematic  
**Primary Thinkers**: Tyler Burge ("Individualism and the Mental", 1979)

In his classic paper *"Individualism and the Mental"* (1979), Tyler Burge presented the **"Arthritis" thought experiment** to establish **Social Externalism** (Anti-Individualism). A patient, Bert, correctly believes that he has had arthritis in his joints for years, but mistakenly believes that his arthritis has spread to his thigh. In a counterfactual society, the medical word "arthritis" is defined by the linguistic community to include rheumatoid ailments of the thigh as well as joints. Bert's internal neurophysiology and physical history are atom-for-atom identical in both worlds. What is Burge's philosophical conclusion?

- (A) Bert's language is a private language in Wittgenstein's sense, proving that medical terminology is logically impossible.
- (B) Bert's internal mental state is completely determined by the firings of his sensory cortex, proving that social communities have no impact on intentionality.
- (C) Bert in the counterfactual world does not have the belief that arthritis has spread to his thigh, because the concept he possesses is determined by the linguistic practices and norms of his community; therefore, mental content does not supervene on the internal physical states of the individual subject ("wide content").
- (D) Bert has committed a category mistake in Ryle's sense, proving that pain does not exist.

**Correct Answer**: **(C)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
Tyler Burge expanded Hilary Putnam's semantic externalism ("'meanings' just ain't in the head!") from natural kind terms (water/$XYZ$) to **propositional attitude content and social linguistic conventions**:
1. **The Scenario**:
   - In the actual world ($W_1$), Bert fears that *"arthritis has spread to my thigh"*. Since arthritis is medically defined strictly as an inflammation of the joints, Bert has a false belief about arthritis.
   - In the counterfactual world ($W_2$), the medical community uses the word "thromcart" (or homophonically "arthritis") to include thigh ailments. Bert has the exact same internal physical brain states and psychological dispositions, but his community's linguistic conventions differ.
   - In $W_2$, Bert does *not* have the concept *arthritis* (the actual joint disease), but a distinct concept (*tharthr*). When he utters the sentence, his belief is true!
2. **The Metaphysical Conclusion**:
   - Since Bert's internal physical state is identical in $W_1$ and $W_2$, but the content of his propositional attitude (his belief) differs, **mental content does not supervene on the internal constitution of the organism**.
   - Mental states are fundamentally relational, constitutively dependent on the linguistic division of labor and communal social norms (**Anti-Individualism**).

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(A) is incorrect**: Burge relies on the communal nature of public language, explicitly rejecting the coherence of private language.
- **(B) is incorrect**: This is the internalist/individualist thesis (held by Jerry Fodor and early Noam Chomsky) which Burge’s thought experiment decisively refutes.
- **(D) is incorrect**: Bert makes an ordinary factual/conceptual error about medical diagnosis, not a Rylean category mistake.

**Primary Textual Reference**: Tyler Burge, "Individualism and the Mental", *Midwest Studies in Philosophy* 4, no. 1 (1979): 73–121; Tyler Burge, *Foundations of Mind: Philosophical Essays, Volume 2* (Oxford University Press, 2007).

### Question 14: Jaegwon Kim's Supervenience Argument and the Causal Exclusion Problem
**Format**: Formal Dialectical Argument (Format C)  
**Difficulty Tier**: Moderate / Systematic  
**Primary Thinkers**: Jaegwon Kim (*Mind in a Physical World*, 1998)

Jaegwon Kim formulated the **Causal Exclusion Argument** to show that Non-Reductive Physicalism faces an inescapable dilemma between epiphenomenalism and overdetermination. Consider a mental event with property $M$ that causes another mental event with property $M^*$. Assume:
1. $M^*$ supervenes on a physical property $P^*$.
2. **The Principle of Causal Closure of the Physical**: Every physical event has a sufficient physical cause.
3. **The Principle of Causal Exclusion**: No single event can have more than one sufficient cause occurring at the same time, unless it is a genuine case of overdetermination.

What is Kim's conclusion regarding the causal power of $M$?

- (A) Mental events operate through telekinesis to violate the laws of physics and overwrite neural firings in the cerebral cortex.
- (B) The exclusion principle proves that the physical world does not exist and that only mental properties have causal efficacy.
- (C) To cause $M^*$, $M$ must cause its supervenience base $P^*$; but by physical closure, $P^*$ already has a sufficient physical cause $P$; therefore, by the exclusion principle, the physical cause $P$ excludes the mental cause $M$, reducing $M$ to a causally impotent epiphenomenon unless $M$ is strictly identical to $P$ (Reductive Physicalism).
- (D) Every event in the universe is causally overdetermined by an infinite chain of non-physical angels.

**Correct Answer**: **(C)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
Jaegwon Kim's Supervenience / Exclusion Argument (*Mind in a Physical World*, 1998; *Physicalism, or Something Near Enough*, 2005) is the most formidable challenge to non-reductive physicalism:
1. **The Setup**:
   - Non-reductive physicalists claim that mental property $M$ is distinct from physical property $P$, yet $M$ causes another mental property $M^*$.
2. **The Downward Step**:
   - $M^*$ is realized by physical base $P^*$. To bring about $M^*$, $M$ must bring about its realizer $P^*$ (downward causation).
3. **The Causal Clash**:
   - By the **Causal Closure of the Physical**, $P^*$ must have a fully sufficient antecedent physical cause, $P$.
   - Now we have two purported causes of $P^*$: the physical event $P$ and the mental event $M$.
4. **The Exclusion Principle**:
   - Is $P^*$ systematically overdetermined (like two bullets striking a victim's heart at the exact same millisecond)? No; mental causation cannot be a pervasive cosmic coincidence of double-causation.
   - Therefore, the physical cause $P$ preempts and excludes the mental property $M$.
   - **The Dilemma**: Non-reductive physicalists must either surrender mental causation (conceding Epiphenomenalism) or identify $M$ with $P$ (surrendering Non-Reductionism).

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(A) is incorrect**: Violates the principle of physical closure, which Kim accepts as fundamental to physicalism.
- **(B) is incorrect**: Reverses the conclusion; Kim’s argument shows the dominance of physical causation over mental causation, not vice-versa.
- **(D) is incorrect**: Kim explicitly rejects pervasive causal overdetermination as metaphysically absurd.

**Primary Textual Reference**: Jaegwon Kim, *Mind in a Physical World: An Essay on the Mind-Body Problem and Mental Causation* (MIT Press, 1998), Chapter 2 & 3; Jaegwon Kim, *Physicalism, or Something Near Enough* (Princeton University Press, 2005).

### Question 15: Daniel Dennett's Intentional Stance and Mild Realism
**Format**: Conceptual Multiple-Choice (Format A)  
**Difficulty Tier**: Moderate / Systematic  
**Primary Thinkers**: Daniel Dennett (*The Intentional Stance*, 1987)

In *The Intentional Stance* (1987), Daniel Dennett proposes a pragmatic, interpretivist strategy for predicting and explaining the behavior of complex systems. He outlines three predictive stances:
1. The **Physical Stance** (using physics and chemistry).
2. The **Design Stance** (using functional purposes and engineering blueprints).
3. The **Intentional Stance** (treating the system as a rational agent with beliefs, desires, and goals).

What is Dennett's position on the ontological status of beliefs and desires (**"Mild Realism"**)?

- (A) The intentional stance can only be applied to adult human beings who have passed a written test in propositional logic.
- (B) Beliefs are immaterial Cartesian substances that exist in a spiritual realm beyond the reach of scientific prediction.
- (C) Beliefs and desires are completely fictitious illusions with no predictive validity, and should be eliminated from scientific language immediately.
- (D) Beliefs and desires are not discrete, language-like neurological objects etched into the cerebral cortex (as Jerry Fodor’s Language of Thought maintains), but real, objective patterns in the observable behavior of an agent that can be reliably detected and predicted from the intentional stance, analogous to centers of gravity in physics.

**Correct Answer**: **(D)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
Daniel Dennett's *The Intentional Stance* (1987) and "Real Patterns" (1991) navigate a careful middle path between Fodorian hyper-realism and Churchlandian eliminativism:
1. **The Three Stances**:
   - To predict an alarm clock or a falling boulder, we use the *physical stance*.
   - To predict a computer running software or a biological heart, we use the *design stance*.
   - To predict a chess-playing computer, a thermostat, a dog, or a human being, we adopt the *intentional stance*: we attribute the beliefs and desires the system *ought* to have given its place in the world, and assume it will act rationally to satisfy them.
2. **Mild Realism vs. Industrial-Strength Realism**:
   - Jerry Fodor argued that beliefs are physically discrete sentence-tokens in a neurobiological "Language of Thought" (Mentalese). Dennett rejects this.
   - But Dennett also rejects Eliminative Materialism: beliefs are not fictions.
   - Instead, beliefs are **real patterns**: just as a "center of gravity" is an abstract mathematical reality that genuinely explains and predicts planetary motion without being a physical marble inside the planet, beliefs are real behavioral patterns visible only from the intentional stance.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(A) is incorrect**: Dennett applies the intentional stance to thermostats, chess computers, and animals, not exclusively to human logicians.
- **(B) is incorrect**: Dennett is a staunch physicalist; he utterly rejects Cartesian dualism.
- **(C) is incorrect**: This is Paul and Patricia Churchland's radical Eliminative Materialism, which Dennett explicitly disputes.

**Primary Textual Reference**: Daniel C. Dennett, *The Intentional Stance* (MIT Press, 1987); Daniel C. Dennett, "Real Patterns", *Journal of Philosophy* 88, no. 1 (1991): 27–51.
