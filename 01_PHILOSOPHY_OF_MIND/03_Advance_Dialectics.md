### Question 1: Jaegwon Kim's Causal Exclusion Problem for Non-Reductive Physicalism
**Format**: Conceptual Multiple-Choice (Format A)  
**Difficulty Tier**: Advance / Dialectical  
**Primary Thinkers**: Jaegwon Kim (1998, 2005)

In *Mind in a Physical World* (1998) and *Physicalism, or Something Near Enough* (2005), Jaegwon Kim formulates the **Causal Exclusion Argument** to demonstrate that Non-Reductive Physicalism collapses into either epiphenomenalism or full-blown reductive type physicalism. Consider the classic tetrad of physicalist commitments:
1. *Mind-Body Supervenience*: Mental properties supervene on physical properties.
2. *Mental Realism / Efficacy*: Mental events make a genuine causal difference in the physical world.
3. *Causal Closure of the Physical Domain*: If a physical event has a cause at time $t$, it has a complete physical cause at $t$.
4. *No Overdetermination*: Normal physical events are not systematically overdetermined by two independent, sufficient causes.

How does Kim demonstrate that these four principles force the Non-Reductive Physicalist to render the mental causally inert (Epiphenomenalism)?

- (A) Suppose a mental event $M$ causes another mental event $M^*$. By supervenience, $M^*$ must have a physical supervenience base $P^*$. To cause $M^*$, $M$ must cause its physical base $P^*$. But by Causal Closure, $P^*$ already has a complete, sufficient physical cause $P$. Unless we accept absurd, pervasive overdetermination, the physical cause $P$ completely excludes and preempts $M$, leaving $M$ with no independent causal work to do.
- (B) Because non-reductive physicalism denies that mental events have spatial mass, it violates Einstein's General Relativity equation $E=mc^2$.
- (C) Because supervenience is a symmetric relation, physical events must supervene on mental events, causing an infinite regress of causal attributions.
- (D) Because quantum indeterminacy at the synaptic cleft prevents brain states from obeying deterministic physical laws.

**Correct Answer**: **(A)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
Kim’s Causal Exclusion Argument is widely regarded as the most formidable internal challenge to non-reductive physicalism (such as Davidson's anomalous monism or functionalist token identity). 

The derivation proceeds as follows:
1. Suppose mental state $M$ (e.g., a desire to drink water) causes mental state $M^*$ (an intention to reach for the glass).
2. By the **Mind-Body Supervenience** thesis, mental properties cannot float freely; $M^*$ is instantiated at time $t$ because its physical base $P^*$ (a motor cortex activation) is instantiated. Thus, to cause $M^*$, $M$ must bring about $P^*$.
3. However, by the **Causal Closure of the Physical**, any physical event like $P^*$ has a *sufficient physical cause* at time $t-1$, which we call $P$ (the firing of premotor neurons).
4. Now consider $P^*$: it has a physical cause $P$, and a putative mental cause $M$. Are both causes operating? By the **Principle of No Overdetermination**, we cannot claim that every single voluntary action in human life is systematically overdetermined (like two bullets striking a heart at the exact same millisecond).
5. If $P$ is physically sufficient to cause $P^*$, what causal work is left for $M$? None. $P$ preempts and excludes $M$.
6. Therefore, either $M$ is strictly identical to $P$ (**Reductive Physicalism**), or $M$ is a causally impotent shadow cast by $P$ (**Epiphenomenalism**). The non-reductivist cannot have both mental distinctness and mental efficacy.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(B) is incorrect**: The exclusion argument does not rely on relativistic mass equations; it is an argument based on the completeness of physics, supervenience, and the rejection of systematic overdetermination.
- **(C) is incorrect**: Supervenience is explicitly an asymmetric or non-symmetric dependency relation ($B$ supervenes on $A$ does not mean $A$ supervenes on $B$); Kim never assumes mental-to-physical supervenience.
- **(D) is incorrect**: Appealing to quantum mechanics does not rescue non-reductive physicalism, because even in stochastic physics, probabilities are governed by physical wavefunctions, leaving no room for distinct non-physical mental intervention.

**Primary Textual Reference**: Jaegwon Kim, *Mind in a Physical World: An Essay on the Mind-Body Problem and Mental Causation* (MIT Press, 1998), Chapter 2: "The Causal Exclusion Problem", pp. 29–56.

### Question 2: David Chalmers's Two-Dimensional Semantics and the Zombie Argument
**Format**: Conceptual Multiple-Choice (Format A)  
**Difficulty Tier**: Advance / Dialectical  
**Primary Thinkers**: David J. Chalmers (1996, 2002)

In *The Conscious Mind* (1996), David Chalmers formulates the **Philosophical Zombie Argument** against physicalism:
1. It is conceivable that there is a creature physically and functionally identical to me, but lacking any conscious experience (*qualia*).
2. If it is conceivable that there is a physical duplicate of me without consciousness, it is **metaphysically possible** that there is such a creature.
3. If it is metaphysically possible that there is a physical duplicate without consciousness, then consciousness is not supervenient on the physical.
4. Therefore, physicalism is false.

Type-B physicalists (such as Brian Loar and Ned Block) respond by invoking Saul Kripke’s classic examples of the **necessary *a posteriori***: just as *"Water is H₂O"* is conceivable to be false (e.g., imagining water made of XYZ) yet metaphysically necessary, so *"Pain is C-fiber firing"* is conceivable to be false yet metaphysically necessary. How does Chalmers’s **Two-Dimensional Semantics** refute this Type-B physicalist response?

- (A) He demonstrates that while *"Water is H₂O"* involves a gap between primary intension (appearance) and secondary intension (underlying chemical essence), for phenomenal concepts like *pain*, **appearance is essence**—its primary intension and secondary intension coincide, meaning that primary conceivability directly entails secondary metaphysical possibility.
- (B) He argues that Kripke was mistaken about rigid designation, and that proper names are actually descriptive definite descriptions in all modal worlds.
- (C) He proves that water is not chemically identical to H₂O in modal counterfactuals containing heavy water ($D_2O$).
- (D) He shows that zombies are technologically realizable through advanced optogenetic CRISPR gene editing.

**Correct Answer**: **(A)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
Type-B Physicalism attempts to save physicalism by driving a wedge between **epistemic conceivability** and **metaphysical possibility**. They point to Kripke’s *Naming and Necessity*: Before modern chemistry, people could conceive of water not being H₂O (e.g., XYZ on Putnam's Twin Earth). Yet *"Water is H₂O"* is metaphysically necessary across all possible worlds. Similarly, Type-B physicalists claim: "We can *conceive* of a zombie, but that only reflects our conceptual limitations; metaphysically, any physical duplicate is necessarily conscious."

Chalmers deploys **Two-Dimensional Semantics** to dismantle this escape route:
Every expression has two distinct semantic dimensions (intensions):
1. *Primary Intension (Epistemic)*: Maps a world considered as *actual* to an extension. For 'water', the primary intension is "the watery stuff in our environment" (clear, drinkable, odorless liquid).
2. *Secondary Intension (Metaphysical)*: Maps a world considered as *counterfactual* to an extension, fixed by the actual world. For 'water', its secondary intension is H₂O.

In the case of "Water is XYZ", there is an epistemic/metaphysical gap *only because* the primary intension (watery stuff) diverges from the secondary intension (H₂O molecular structure). 

However, in the case of **phenomenal concepts** (qualia like *pain* or *redness*), there is no hidden microscopic essence beneath the surface appearance:
> "With phenomenal concepts, appearance is reality. The phenomenal feel of pain *is* the essence of pain. For phenomenal concepts, the primary and secondary intensions are identical."

Because the primary and secondary intensions coincide, the Kripkean gap between conceivability and possibility vanishes:
$$\text{Primary Conceivability of } (P \land \neg Q) \implies \text{Metaphysical Possibility of } (P \land \neg Q)$$
Therefore, since zombies are primarily conceivable, they are metaphysically possible, and physicalism is refuted.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(B) is incorrect**: Chalmers enthusiastically adopts Kripke's rigid designation framework; Two-Dimensional semantics is designed to formalize Kripke's modal insights, not reject them.
- **(C) is incorrect**: Heavy water ($D_2O$) is a peripheral chemical trivia that has nothing to do with the semantic distinction between primary and secondary intensions.
- **(D) is incorrect**: Philosophical zombies are not physically or biologically possible creatures (they violate physical laws); Chalmers’s argument is strictly about *metaphysical / logical possibility* ($P \land \neg Q$).

**Primary Textual Reference**: David J. Chalmers, *The Conscious Mind: In Search of a Fundamental Theory* (Oxford University Press, 1996), Chapter 4: "Naturalistic Dualism", pp. 131–171; and Chalmers, "Does Conceivability Entail Possibility?", in *Conceivability and Possibility*, ed. T. Gendler and J. Hawthorne (Oxford University Press, 2002), pp. 145–200.

### Question 3: The Extended Mind and Active Externalism
**Format**: Conceptual Multiple-Choice (Format A)  
**Difficulty Tier**: Advance / Dialectical  
**Primary Thinkers**: Andy Clark & David Chalmers (1998)

In their seminal 1998 paper "The Extended Mind", Andy Clark and David Chalmers formulate the thesis of **Active Externalism** using the famous contrast between **Inga** and **Otto**. Otto suffers from Alzheimer's disease and relies on a notebook where he writes down directions, addresses, and museum locations. What is the **Parity Principle** formulated by Clark and Chalmers, and what exact criteria must Otto's notebook meet to count as part of his cognitive apparatus?

- (A) The Parity Principle states: *"If, as we confront some task, a part of the world functions as a process which, were it done in the head, we would have no hesitation in recognizing as part of the cognitive process, then that part of the world is part of the cognitive process"*; and the notebook must be constantly accessible, automatically endorsed, and previously verified.
- (B) The Parity Principle states that human brains and electronic computers have equal ethical rights under international human rights law.
- (C) The Parity Principle claims that internal neural states are completely identical in spatial coordinates to the external objects they perceive.
- (D) The Parity Principle asserts that Otto’s notebook contains phenomenal qualia because cellulose fibers store subjective visual representations.

**Correct Answer**: **(A)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
Clark and Chalmers distinguish their "Active Externalism" from Hilary Putnam's and Tyler Burge's traditional semantic externalism. Traditional externalism is passive and distal: what words mean depends on historical causal chains or social conventions (e.g., Twin Earth). Active externalism, by contrast, claims that **the physical machinery of mind itself extends beyond the skin and skull into the external environment**.

They illustrate this with the Inga/Otto thought experiment:
- *Inga* hears about an exhibition at the Museum of Modern Art on 53rd Street. She pauses, consults her biological memory, and walks to 53rd Street.
- *Otto* has Alzheimer's disease. He records all new information in a notebook. When he hears about the exhibition, he consults his notebook, finds the entry "MoMA is on 53rd Street," and walks there.

Clark and Chalmers introduce the **Parity Principle**:
> "If, as we confront some task, a part of the world functions as a process which, were it done in the head, we would have no hesitation in recognizing as part of the cognitive process, then that part of the world is part of the cognitive process."

To prevent trivialization (e.g., claiming the entire Internet or telephone directory is part of my mind), Clark and Chalmers specify strict boundary conditions for Otto's notebook:
1. *Constancy*: The notebook is a constant, readily accessible part of Otto's life.
2. *Automatic Endorsement*: Any information retrieved from the notebook is automatically trusted, without external skeptical cross-examination (just as Inga trusts her biological memory).
3. *Past Conscious Endorsement*: The information was consciously endorsed and recorded by Otto himself.

Under these conditions, Otto's notebook functions identically to Inga's biological non-occurrent beliefs, proving that beliefs and cognitive processes can reside outside the biological brain.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(B) is incorrect**: The Extended Mind thesis is an ontological and cognitive thesis about the boundary of mind, not an ethical doctrine about legal human rights for computers.
- **(C) is incorrect**: Active externalism does not claim neural states have the same spatial coordinates as external objects; it claims the cognitive process *includes* the external loop as a coupled system.
- **(D) is incorrect**: Clark and Chalmers explicitly restrict the Extended Mind thesis to **functional, cognitive, and intentional states** (beliefs, memories, computational processing); they explicitly bracket phenomenal qualia, acknowledging that phenomenal consciousness may remain internal to the nervous system.

**Primary Textual Reference**: Andy Clark and David J. Chalmers, "The Extended Mind", *Analysis* 58(1) (1998): 7–19.

### Question 4: Ned Block on the Double Dissociation of Phenomenal and Access Consciousness
**Format**: Conceptual Multiple-Choice (Format A)  
**Difficulty Tier**: Advance / Dialectical  
**Primary Thinkers**: Ned Block ("On a Confusion about a Function of Consciousness", 1995)

In his landmark 1995 target article in *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*, Ned Block argued that the contemporary consciousness debate is paralyzed because researchers conflate two radically different concepts of consciousness: **Phenomenal Consciousness ($P$-consciousness)** and **Access Consciousness ($A$-consciousness)**. What is the precise conceptual definition of these two types, and how does Block argue for their potential **double dissociation**?

- (A) $P$-consciousness is raw subjective phenomenal experience ("what it is like" to feel pain or see red); $A$-consciousness is functional availability for cognitive use (a representation is $A$-conscious if it is poised for direct use in reasoning, reporting, and executive voluntary control of action); they are doubly dissociable because a subject can have $P$-experience without cognitive access (e.g., unattended background drill noise), and theoretically $A$-access without phenomenal experience (e.g., a philosophical super-blindsighter).
- (B) $P$-consciousness exists only in primates, while $A$-consciousness exists exclusively in artificial intelligence software.
- (C) Block argues that $P$-consciousness is an illusion that does not exist, leaving only $A$-consciousness in physical nature.
- (D) $P$-consciousness represents the peripheral nervous system, while $A$-consciousness represents the autonomic nervous system.

**Correct Answer**: **(A)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
Block's distinction is one of the most fundamental conceptual tools in contemporary philosophy of mind and cognitive neuroscience:
1. **Phenomenal Consciousness ($P$-Consciousness)**:
   - Experiential, qualitative properties: *qualia*.
   - What it is like to be in that state (Nagel). Sensory sensations (hearing a flute, tasting sugar, seeing scarlet), bodily sensations (pains, tickles), and emotional feelings.
   - It is defined by its subjective phenomenology, not its functional role.
2. **Access Consciousness ($A$-Consciousness)**:
   - A computational and functional information-processing concept.
   - Block defines a representation as $A$-conscious if it is:
     1. Poised for direct control of voluntary thought and action;
     2. Poised for rational guidance in reasoning;
     3. Poised for verbal report.
3. **The Argument for Double Dissociation**:
   - **Dissociation 1: $P$-without-$A$**:
     - *Block's Background Noise Case*: You are intensely engaged in a philosophical conversation. Nearby, an electric drill has been loudly whining outside the window for an hour. Suddenly, you notice: *"The drill has been making noise all afternoon!"*
     - Block argues that you were phenomenally experiencing the sound all along ($P$-conscious), but until your attention shifted, the representation was not access-conscious ($A$-conscious): it was not poised for reasoning or reporting.
     - Sperling's (1960) iconic memory experiments also suggest that visual phenomenal richness exceeds what can be accessed and reported in working memory.
   - **Dissociation 2: $A$-without-$P$**:
     - *The Super-Blindsighter Thought Experiment*: Real blindsight patients (Weiskrantz) have visual cortex V1 damage. When forced to guess, they identify the orientation of visual stimuli with 90% accuracy, yet they adamantly report that they see nothing phenomenally.
     - Block imagines a hypothetical "super-blindsighter" who has trained herself to spontaneously use her visual data without prompting: if asked "is there an X on the screen?", the information pops into her head and she answers accurately and acts on it voluntarily ($A$-conscious), yet she experiences **zero visual qualia** (no $P$-consciousness).
   - This proves that cognitive access and phenomenal experience are conceptually independent.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(B) is incorrect**: Block applies both concepts across cognitive systems; the distinction is metaphysical and conceptual, not a phylogenetic division between primates and software.
- **(C) is incorrect**: Dennett and eliminativists deny $P$-consciousness; Block is a passionate realist about $P$-consciousness and formulated the distinction precisely to protect phenomenal qualia from being reduced to functional access.
- **(D) is incorrect**: Conflates cognitive-philosophical concepts of conscious awareness with biological neuroanatomy (peripheral vs. autonomic pathways).

**Primary Textual Reference**: Ned Block, "On a Confusion about a Function of Consciousness", *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 18, no. 2 (1995): 227–247; Ned Block, "Comparing the Major Theories of Consciousness", in *The Cambridge Handbook of Consciousness*, ed. M. Moscovitch et al. (Cambridge University Press, 2007).

### Question 5: Sydney Shoemaker and the Impossibility of "Self-Blindness"
**Format**: Conceptual Multiple-Choice (Format A)  
**Difficulty Tier**: Advance / Dialectical  
**Primary Thinkers**: Sydney Shoemaker ("Self-Knowledge and 'Inner Sense'", 1994)

In his John Locke Lectures ("Self-Knowledge and 'Inner Sense'", 1994), Sydney Shoemaker mounted a famous critique of the **"Inner Sense" theory of introspective self-knowledge** (advocated by John Locke, David Armstrong, and William Lycan). To dismantle the inner-sense model, Shoemaker introduced the concept of **"Self-Blindness"**. What is self-blindness, and why does Shoemaker argue that it is conceptually impossible for a rational creature?

- (A) Self-blindness is the hypothetical condition of a rational creature who has normal beliefs, desires, and conceptual capacities, but completely lacks an "inner sense" to perceive its own mental states; Shoemaker proves this is conceptually impossible because any being with full rationality, mastery of concepts, and the capacity for deliberation must necessarily know its own mental states without needing a secondary sensory organ, disproving the inner-sense model.
- (B) Self-blindness is a neurological disorder caused by damage to the optic chiasm that prevents patients from recognizing their own reflection in a mirror.
- (C) Shoemaker argues that human beings are completely blind to their own moral failings due to unconscious Freudian ego defense mechanisms.
- (D) Self-blindness proves that functionalism cannot explain why humans have two eyes instead of one.

**Correct Answer**: **(A)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
1. **The Inner Sense Model**:
   - Thinkers like Armstrong and Lycan claim that introspection is like an "inner eye" or inner sensory organ that perceives first-order mental states (just as the physical eye perceives external tables and chairs).
   - *Shoemaker's Insight*: If introspection is a perceptual sense mechanism, then just as a person can have normal intellectual faculties while lacking physical vision (physical blindness), a person should be able to have normal intellectual faculties while lacking inner sense: **Self-Blindness**.
2. **The Definition of a Self-Blind Creature**:
   - A creature who has beliefs, desires, pains, and full conceptual rationality, but lacks the perceptual organ of inner sense, and thus cannot introspect its own mental states.
3. **Shoemaker's Reductio Ad Absurdum**:
   - Could a self-blind rational creature exist? **No!**
   - Suppose the creature believes that it is raining ($p$).
   - Because the creature is fully rational, if you ask it: *"Do you believe that it is raining?"*, what will it do?
   - Following Gareth Evans's transparency method, to determine whether it believes $p$, the creature simply looks out the window at the weather. Seeing rain, it assents to $p$.
   - A rational creature will not assert Moore's Paradox: *"It is raining, but I don't believe it."*
   - To act rationally, revise beliefs in light of evidence, and deliberate about choices, the creature **must automatically behave and speak as if it knows its own beliefs and desires**.
   - If an agent is cognitively and behaviorally indistinguishable from someone who has self-knowledge, then there is no sense in which it lacks self-knowledge.
   - Therefore, self-knowledge is not a perceptual causal monitoring mechanism (inner sense); it is an **intrinsic constitutive feature of rational agency**.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(B) is incorrect**: Mirror self-unrecognition is *prosopagnosia* or *mirror agnosia*; Shoemaker's self-blindness is a philosophical thought experiment about introspective belief-awareness, not visual face perception.
- **(C) is incorrect**: Moral self-deception and psychoanalysis are empirical psychological issues, entirely distinct from Shoemaker's analytic epistemological critique of perceptual models of self-knowledge.
- **(D) is incorrect**: The number of biological eyes has zero relevance to analytic philosophy of introspection and mental representation.

**Primary Textual Reference**: Sydney Shoemaker, "Self-Knowledge and 'Inner Sense': Lecture I: The Object Perception Model; Lecture II: The Broad Perceptual Model; Lecture III: The Phenomenal Character of Experience", *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research* 54, no. 2 (1994): 249–314; Sydney Shoemaker, *The First-Person Perspective and Other Essays* (Cambridge University Press, 1996).

### Question 6: John McDowell: The "Space of Reasons" and the Unboundedness of the Conceptual
**Format**: Conceptual Multiple-Choice (Format A)  
**Difficulty Tier**: Advance / Dialectical  
**Primary Thinkers**: John McDowell (*Mind and World*, 1994), Wilfrid Sellars (1956), Immanuel Kant

In *Mind and World* (1994), John McDowell addressed the deep epistemological crisis regarding how thought can bear upon an objective reality without falling into either the **"Myth of the Given"** (Sellars) or **"Coherentism as Frictionless Spinning in the Void"** (Rorty/Davidson). How does McDowell’s neo-Kantian synthesis resolve this dilemma through his conception of the **"Space of Reasons"** and passive perceptual experience?

- (A) In perceptual experience, conceptual capacities are already passively drawn into operation; sensory impressions are not pre-conceptual brute physical impacts that somehow justify beliefs (the Myth of the Given), but neither is thought a self-contained web disconnected from nature: conceptual capacities are operative in perceptual experience itself, showing that the space of reasons is unbounded and that nature is open to empirical friction.
- (B) McDowell argues that the external physical world does not exist and that the universe is made entirely of subjective linguistic grammar.
- (C) The space of reasons refers to the physical volume of outer space between the Earth and Mars.
- (D) McDowell claims that empirical science must be abolished in favor of medieval religious scholasticism.

**Correct Answer**: **(A)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
McDowell's *Mind and World* is widely considered one of the most important philosophical texts of the late 20th century, attempting to rescue empirical realism from an intolerable oscillation:
1. **Horn 1: The Myth of the Given (Sellars 1956)**:
   - Foundationalism tries to ground knowledge in raw, pre-conceptual sensory inputs (the "Given").
   - *Sellars/McDowell refutation*: A raw causal impact of light on the retina is merely an event in the **"realm of law"** (mechanical nature). A physical cause cannot serve as a **justification or reason** for a belief. Causes produce effects; only propositions in the space of reasons can justify beliefs. Conflating causal impact with epistemic justification is the Myth of the Given.
2. **Horn 2: Coherentism (Davidson / Rorty)**:
   - To avoid the Given, coherentists claim that *"nothing can count as a reason for holding a belief except another belief."*
   - *McDowell's refutation*: If beliefs are only justified by other beliefs within an enclosed linguistic web, human thought loses all objective contact with the real world. Mind becomes **"a frictionless spinning in a void"**, detached from physical reality.
3. **McDowell's Kantian Solution**:
   - Revives Kant's dictum: *"Thoughts without content are empty; intuitions without concepts are blind."*
   - McDowell argues that in human adult perception, **conceptual capacities are already active in perceptual experience itself**.
   - Perception is not a two-stage process (first a raw dumb sensation, then a conceptual judgment slapped onto it). Rather, our visual experience is *already conceptualized*: we passively see *that the wall is yellow*.
   - Because experience is already conceptual, it can stand in rational, justificatory relations to our beliefs (providing epistemic friction), while remaining receptive to the world. The Space of Reasons reaches all the way to the boundary of our sensory receptivity.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(B) is incorrect**: McDowell is an empirical realist who insists that nature provides real epistemic friction on human thought; he explicitly rejects Rortyan anti-realism and linguistic idealism.
- **(C) is incorrect**: A literal, humorous spatial misinterpretation of Sellars's epistemological phrase "the logical space of reasons".
- **(D) is incorrect**: McDowell is an analytical philosopher synthesizing Aristotle, Kant, and Frege to defend rational empiricism, not medieval scholasticism.

**Primary Textual Reference**: John McDowell, *Mind and World* (Harvard University Press, 1994), Lectures I and II; Wilfrid Sellars, *Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind* (Harvard University Press, 1956/1997).

### Question 7: Ruth Garrett Millikan's Teleosemantics: Proper Functions and Consumer Devices
**Format**: Conceptual Multiple-Choice (Format A)  
**Difficulty Tier**: Advance / Dialectical  
**Primary Thinkers**: Ruth Garrett Millikan (*Language, Thought, and Other Biological Categories*, 1984), Fred Dretske (1988)

In *Language, Thought, and Other Biological Categories* (1984), Ruth Garrett Millikan formulated **Biosemantics (Teleosemantics)** to naturalize intentionality and solve the problem of mental misrepresentation. How does Millikan's teleosemantics determine the semantic content of a mental representation via the concept of **"Proper Functions"** and **"Consumer Mechanisms"**?

- (A) The content of a mental representation is determined not by what typically causes it, but by the evolutionary **proper function** of the **consumer device** that interprets and acts upon the representation: the content is that condition of the environment which historically accounted for the evolutionary survival success of the organism when the consumer mechanism performed its function.
- (B) Millikan argues that semantic content is dictated entirely by dictionaries approved by government ministries of education.
- (C) Proper functions are defined as the mathematical equations calculated by graphics processing units during video rendering.
- (D) Teleosemantics claims that mental representations only exist in plant roots during photosynthesis.

**Correct Answer**: **(A)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
1. **The Crisis of Causal Theories of Content (Fodor's Disjunction Problem)**:
   - Simple causal theories claim: representation $R$ means $X$ because $X$ causes $R$ (e.g., horse-token means "horse" because horses cause it).
   - *The Failure*: If a cow on a foggy night causes the agent to think "horse", the simple causal theory must conclude that "horse" means "horse-or-cow-on-foggy-night". Simple causal theories cannot account for **misrepresentation** (error).
2. **Millikan's Teleological Breakthrough**:
   - Millikan shifts focus from the **producer** of the representation to the **consumer**:
     ```
     [Environment] ──> [Producer Mechanism] ──(Representation)──> [Consumer Mechanism] ──> [Adaptive Behavior]
     ```
   - An organ or cognitive mechanism has a **Proper Function** if its historical ancestors performed that effect, and that performance accounts for why the mechanism was selected by natural selection and reproduced today (e.g., the proper function of the heart is to pump blood, even if a diseased heart currently fails to pump blood).
3. **Consumer-Based Semantic Content**:
   - The consumer device takes the internal representation as an input and produces adaptive behavior.
   - For the consumer device to successfully perform its proper function, certain conditions in the environment must hold.
   - **The Semantic Rule**: The content of the representation is **whatever state of the environment must be the case** in order for the consumer device to succeed in its evolutionary proper function.
   - Example: A beaver's tail-slap represents "danger/predator nearby" because the other beavers (the consumers) dive underwater. The diving behavior only achieves its proper function (survival) if a predator is actually present. If a falling branch causes a tail-slap, the beaver made an evolutionary misrepresentation; the slap still meant "predator", but was false.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(B) is incorrect**: Teleosemantics is grounded in evolutionary biological adaptation and natural selection, not conventional human dictionaries or legal bureaucracies.
- **(C) is incorrect**: Conflates biological teleology with computer graphics GPU mathematics.
- **(D) is incorrect**: Millikan applies teleosemantics primarily to animal and human cognitive systems, not plant root botany.

**Primary Textual Reference**: Ruth Garrett Millikan, *Language, Thought, and Other Biological Categories: New Foundations for Realism* (MIT Press, 1984); Ruth Garrett Millikan, "Biosemantics", *Journal of Philosophy* 86, no. 6 (1989): 281–297; Fred Dretske, *Explaining Behavior: Reasons in a World of Causes* (MIT Press, 1988).

### Question 8: Galen Strawson's "A Priori" Argument for Realistic Monism and Panpsychism
**Format**: Conceptual Multiple-Choice (Format A)  
**Difficulty Tier**: Advance / Dialectical  
**Primary Thinkers**: Galen Strawson ("Realistic Monism: Why Physicalism Entails Panpsychism", 2006)

In his provocative paper "Realistic Monism: Why Physicalism Entails Panpsychism" (*Journal of Consciousness Studies*, 2006), Galen Strawson argues that any philosopher who is a genuine, thoroughgoing physicalist must inevitably embrace **Panpsychism**. What is Strawson's core argument regarding the impossibility of **"Radical Emergence"**?

- (A) Experientiality (phenomenal consciousness) cannot emerge out of wholly non-experiential matter, because experientiality cannot be intelligibly derived from, constituted by, or understood in terms of the purely non-experiential; since conscious experience undeniably exists and physicalism claims everything is physical, the fundamental constituents of physical reality must themselves be experiential, meaning physicalism strictly entails panpsychism.
- (B) Strawson argues that physical matter is made of tiny plastic spheres manufactured in industrial factories.
- (C) The argument asserts that human consciousness was brought to Earth by extraterrestrial spacecraft 10,000 years ago.
- (D) Strawson claims that physicalism is false and that the only reality is Cartesian substance dualism.

**Correct Answer**: **(A)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
Galen Strawson takes physicalism more seriously than traditional reductionists:
1. **Real Physicalism Must Acknowledge the Hardest Fact**:
   - Strawson defines "Real Physicalism" as the claim that every real concrete phenomenon is physical.
   - The most undeniable, directly known concrete phenomenon in the universe is **experiential consciousness** (*qualia*, feeling).
   - Therefore, a genuine physicalist cannot deny that experience is real and physical.
2. **The Impossibility of Radical Emergence**:
   - Standard materialists claim that consciousness is an "emergent property" of complex brains, just as liquidity is an emergent property of $H_2O$ molecules.
   - *Strawson's Critique*: Liquidity is **intelligible emergence**: liquidity is simply the spatial-kinetic behavior of non-rigid molecular bonds sliding past each other. The micro-properties ($H_2O$ molecules, dipole attraction) conceptually and physically constitute the macro-property (liquidity).
   - In contrast, experientiality emerging from totally non-experiential matter would be **Radical / Brutal Emergence**: getting something completely new out of a domain that contains zero trace of it.
   - Strawson argues: *"You cannot get experientiality out of non-experientiality. It is as impossible as getting spatial extension out of something that has no spatial properties whatsoever."* If you start with 100% dead, unfeeling, unconscious matter, no amount of complex mechanical rearrangement can suddenly burst into subjective feeling. That would be creation *ex nihilo* (magic).
3. **The Conclusion**:
   - If radical emergence is impossible, and physicalism is true, and experience exists, then **experience must have been present at the bottom level of physical reality all along**.
   - The ultimate physical components of the universe (the fundamental fields, particles, or energy) are experiential in their intrinsic nature. Hence: **Physicalism entails Panpsychism**.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(B) is incorrect**: Strawson discusses fundamental theoretical physics and metaphysics, not synthetic plastics or manufacturing.
- **(C) is incorrect**: Extraterrestrial panspermia does not solve the metaphysical problem of the emergence of experientiality from matter.
- **(D) is incorrect**: Strawson explicitly describes himself as an uncompromising physicalist monist (*Realistic Monism*); he fiercely rejects Cartesian substance dualism.

**Primary Textual Reference**: Galen Strawson, "Realistic Monism: Why Physicalism Entails Panpsychism", *Journal of Consciousness Studies* 13, no. 10–11 (2006): 3–31; Galen Strawson, *Selves: An Essay in Personal Identity* (Oxford University Press, 2009).

### Question 9: Ned Block's Inverted Earth and the Severing of Representation from Qualia
**Format**: Conceptual Multiple-Choice (Format A)  
**Difficulty Tier**: Advance / Dialectical  
**Primary Thinkers**: Ned Block ("Inverted Earth", *Philosophical Perspectives*, 1990)

In "Inverted Earth" (1990), Ned Block crafted an intricate thought experiment to refute **Strong Representationalism / Intentionalism** (the view held by Michael Tye and Fred Dretske that the phenomenal character of an experience is strictly identical to its intentional/representational content). In Block's thought experiment, you are unconsciously transported to Inverted Earth and fitted with color-inverting lenses. How does your long-term adaptation to Inverted Earth prove that phenomenal character cannot be identical to representational content?

- (A) After decades on Inverted Earth, your mental concepts adjust to track the inverted environment, so your visual representations come to represent the inverted worldly colors veridically (e.g., your thought "blue" now represents sky-color, which is physical yellow on Inverted Earth), meaning your representational content has completely shifted 180 degrees; yet because the inverting lenses and inverted environment cancel each other out internally, your subjective phenomenal qualia (the raw qualitative feeling of what it is like to see) remained constant and unchanged throughout, demonstrating that phenomenal character is not identical to representational content.
- (B) Inverted Earth proves that human eyes cannot function when exposed to yellow light waves.
- (C) The thought experiment proves that all people living on Earth are philosophical zombies who lack visual qualia.
- (D) Block argues that color-inverting lenses cause immediate physical amnesia and loss of language comprehension.

**Correct Answer**: **(A)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
Ned Block's *Inverted Earth* is a masterwork of analytical philosophy of mind:
1. **The Opponent: Strong Representationalism (Tye, Dretske, Lycan)**:
   - Strong Representationalism claims that phenomenal consciousness has no independent nature of its own:
     $$\text{Phenomenal Character} \equiv \text{Intentional / Representational Content}$$
   - What it feels like to see blue is simply representing the physical reflectance property of blueness in the environment.
2. **The Inverted Earth Thought Experiment (Block 1990)**:
   - *Earth*: The sky is blue; grass is green. You have normal visual hardware.
   - *Inverted Earth*: The physical colors of objects are complementary opposites of Earth (the sky is yellow; grass is red). However, the English language spoken on Inverted Earth is flipped so that they call the yellow sky "blue" and red grass "green".
   - *The Night of the Abduction*: Mad scientists kidnap you while asleep. They insert color-inverting contact lenses into your eyes and fly you to Inverted Earth.
   - *The Phenomenal Continuity*:
     - When you wake up, you look at the yellow sky through color-inverting lenses. Yellow light passing through an inverting lens hits your retina as blue!
     - You notice **absolutely zero phenomenal change**: the sky looks visually identical to how it looked yesterday on Earth.
   - *The Semantic / Representational Shift Over Time*:
     - By externalist theories of content (Putnam, Burge), mental content tracks the environment the agent is embedded in.
     - After living on Inverted Earth for 50 years, your words and thoughts become causally embedded in Inverted Earth. When you look at the sky and think "That is blue", your word and thought represent the sky-property of Inverted Earth (which is physically yellow). Your **representational content has completely inverted**!
   - *The Decisive Chasm*:
     - Your representational content has flipped from tracking Earth-blue to tracking Inverted-Earth-yellow.
     - But your **phenomenal experience (qualia)** has remained exactly the same throughout!
     - Therefore: **Phenomenal character $\neq$ Representational content**. There are qualitative mental features (*qualia*) that cannot be reduced to externalist semantic tracking.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(B) is incorrect**: Inverting lenses are a standard optical construct in experimental psychology (e.g., Stratton's 1897 prism glasses); human retinas process the full visible spectrum.
- **(C) is incorrect**: The scenario specifically concerns the phenomenal qualia of conscious subjects, not non-conscious zombies.
- **(D) is incorrect**: The thought experiment assumes normal memory and linguistic fluency so the subject can adapt to the community.

**Primary Textual Reference**: Ned Block, "Inverted Earth", *Philosophical Perspectives* 4 (1990): 53–79; Ned Block, "Mental Paint and Mental Latex", *Philosophical Issues* 7 (1996): 19–49; Michael Tye, *Ten Problems of Consciousness* (MIT Press, 1995).

### Question 10: Uriah Kriegel and Self-Representational Theories of Consciousness
**Format**: Conceptual Multiple-Choice (Format A)  
**Difficulty Tier**: Advance / Dialectical  
**Primary Thinkers**: Uriah Kriegel (*Subjective Consciousness*, 2009), Franz Brentano (1874)

In the taxonomy of representational theories of mind, philosophers distinguish between **Higher-Order Theories (HOT)** (David Rosenthal, Peter Carruthers), where a mental state is conscious by virtue of being targeted by a distinct higher-order thought or perception, and **Self-Representational / Same-Order Theories (SOT)** (Uriah Kriegel, Franz Brentano). What is the primary conceptual pathology that Kriegel’s Same-Order Self-Representationalism avoids regarding the threat of **"Targetless HOTs"** and the fragmentation of conscious unity?

- (A) In Higher-Order Theories, because the conscious state $M$ and the higher-order representation $H$ are two separate, distinct mental states located in different brain regions, it is nomologically possible to have a "targetless" higher-order thought ($H$ misrepresents $M$ as occurring when $M$ is absent, or $H$ represents $M$ without $M$ existing), resulting in the absurdity of experiencing a conscious qualitative state that does not exist; Self-Representationalism eliminates this chasm by asserting that a conscious mental state is an integrated, mereological whole that represents the external world while simultaneously **representing itself reflexively** as an intrinsic, same-order constituent.
- (B) Kriegel argues that consciousness is a physical liquid that circulates through the spinal canal.
- (C) Same-order theories claim that human beings are incapable of thinking about mathematics.
- (D) Kriegel agrees with Rosenthal that conscious states are always unconscious until described in an oral telephone conversation.

**Correct Answer**: **(A)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
Uriah Kriegel’s Self-Representationalism provides a rigorous middle path between relational higher-order theories and non-representational qualia realism:
1. **The Higher-Order Dilemma (David Rosenthal's HOT)**:
   - Rosenthal argued: A mental state $M$ (e.g., a pain) is conscious if and only if the subject has a suitable Higher-Order Thought ($H$) about $M$:
     $$H \xrightarrow{\text{represents}} M$$
   - *The Vulnerability (Targetless HOT)*:
     - In any relational theory where $H$ and $M$ are two distinct events, the causal/representational link can fail.
     - Suppose the higher-order center generates an erroneous HOT: *"I am experiencing a severe burning pain in my left wrist"*, but the lower-order sensory pain state $M$ was never activated (a misfire or hallucination in the prefrontal cortex).
     - Does the subject feel pain?
     - If the HOT theorist says *yes*, then the phenomenal character of pain is generated solely by $H$, and $M$ was completely unnecessary (making sensory states irrelevant to consciousness).
     - If the HOT theorist says *no*, then a HOT about pain does not guarantee conscious awareness of pain!
2. **Kriegel's Self-Representational Solution (Brentano's Heir)**:
   - In *Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint* (1874), Franz Brentano noted:
     > *"Every conscious act includes within itself a consciousness of itself... The hearing of a sound has the sound for its primary object, and the hearing itself for its secondary object."*
   - Kriegel (2009) formalizes this into **Self-Representationalism**:
     - A conscious mental state is not two separate states ($H$ and $M$); it is a single, complex mental state that has a dual representational structure:
       1. It represents an external state (e.g., a red apple) as its **primary object**;
       2. It represents **itself** as its **secondary, reflexive object**.
   - Because the self-representation is an intrinsic, inseparable mereological part of the conscious state itself, **a targetless state is logically and structurally impossible**—you cannot have the self-representation without the state being present, because the representation *is* the state representing itself!

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(B) is incorrect**: Kriegel offers an analytic, representational theory of mental content and intentionality, not a hydraulic theory of cerebrospinal fluid.
- **(C) is incorrect**: Self-representationalism is a theory of the phenomenal structure of conscious experience, not a limitation on mathematical cognition.
- **(D) is incorrect**: Rosenthal's HOT theory concerns mental, non-linguistic higher-order thoughts, not spoken telephone conversations.

**Primary Textual Reference**: Uriah Kriegel, *Subjective Consciousness: A Self-Representational Theory* (Oxford University Press, 2009); Franz Brentano, *Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint* (1874; Routledge, 1973); David M. Rosenthal, *Consciousness and Mind* (Clarendon Press / Oxford University Press, 2005).

### Question 11: David Chalmers and Two-Dimensional Semantics: The Conceivability-Possibility Link
**Format**: Modal Epistemology & Formal Dialectics (Format C)  
**Difficulty Tier**: Advance / Dialectical  
**Primary Thinkers**: David J. Chalmers (*The Conscious Mind*, 1996; *The Character of Consciousness*, 2010)

David Chalmers utilizes **Two-Dimensional Semantics** to defend the inference from the ideal conceivability of Philosophical Zombies ($P \land 
eg Q$, where $P$ is all physical truth and $Q$ is phenomenal truth) to their metaphysical possibility. In two-dimensional modal logic, every proposition $S$ has two intensions:
- The **Primary Intension** (1-intension): A function from worlds considered as *actual* to extensions (epistemic).
- The **Secondary Intension** (2-intension): A function from worlds considered as *counterfactual* to extensions (subjunctive/metaphysical).

Why does Kripke's necessary a posteriori (e.g., "Water is $H_2O$") fail to save physicalism from Chalmers' Zombie argument?

- (A) For standard Kripkean scientific identities, the primary and secondary intensions come apart because our epistemic grasp of the concept (e.g., watery stuff) differs from its underlying metaphysical nature ($H_2O$); however, for phenomenal consciousness concepts ($Q$), primary and secondary intensions coincide (they are epistemically transparent), meaning that if $P \land 
eg Q$ is ideally conceptually coherent (1-possible), it is necessarily metaphysically possible (2-possible).
- (B) Because two-dimensional semantics proves that $H_2O$ is not the chemical formula for water in any possible world.
- (C) Because zombies can be physically manufactured in an ordinary biology laboratory using CRISPR gene editing.
- (D) Because Chalmers demonstrates that secondary intensions are meaningless in formal modal logic.

**Correct Answer**: **(A)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
David Chalmers' master argument in *The Conscious Mind* (1996) and *The Character of Consciousness* (2010) uses Two-Dimensional Semantics to refute "Type-B Materialism":
1. **The Type-B Physicalist Defense**:
   - Type-B materialists (e.g., Ned Block, Robert Stalnaker, Brian Loar) argue that just as we can conceive of water without $H_2O$ (because the identity was an empirical discovery), we can conceive of physical brain states without consciousness, but they are nevertheless metaphysically identical.
2. **The 2D Semantics Rejoinder**:
   - Chalmers shows that the conceivability of "Water is not $H_2O$" arises only because the **primary intension** of "water" (the watery stuff in our environment) differs from its **secondary intension** ($H_2O$ across all counterfactual worlds).
   - In contrast, phenomenal concepts have no "accidental epistemic guise". The primary intension of *pain* is *the phenomenal experience of pain*. The secondary intension of *pain* is *the phenomenal experience of pain*.
   - When primary and secondary intensions coincide, **epistemic possibility entails metaphysical possibility**.
   - Therefore, if a complete physical replica of the universe lacking qualia ($P \land 
eg Q$) is ideally conceivable, it is metaphysically possible. And if $P \land 
eg Q$ is metaphysically possible, then phenomenal consciousness does not supervene with metaphysical necessity on the physical world, refuting physicalism.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(B) is incorrect**: Chalmers accepts that "Water is $H_2O$" is metaphysically necessary in the secondary intension; his argument relies on this exact Kripkean foundation.
- **(C) is incorrect**: Zombies are philosophical thought experiments (microphysical duplicates lacking qualia), not biological mutants.
- **(D) is incorrect**: 2D semantics relies heavily on secondary intensions (Kripkean counterfactual evaluation).

**Primary Textual Reference**: David J. Chalmers, *The Conscious Mind: In Search of a Fundamental Theory* (Oxford University Press, 1996), Chapter 4; David J. Chalmers, "The Two-Dimensional Argument Against Materialism", in *The Character of Consciousness* (Oxford University Press, 2010), pp. 141–206.

### Question 12: Sydney Shoemaker's Subset Account of Realization and Causal Powers
**Format**: Conceptual / Dialectical (Format C)  
**Difficulty Tier**: Advance / Dialectical  
**Primary Thinkers**: Sydney Shoemaker (*Physical Realization*, 2007)

To defend non-reductive physicalism from Jaegwon Kim's Causal Exclusion Problem, Sydney Shoemaker proposed the **Subset Account of Realization**. How does defining property realization in terms of conditional causal powers resolve the threat of causal exclusion?

- (A) A mental property $M$ is realized by a physical property $P$ if and only if the set of causal powers conferred by $M$ is a proper **subset** of the causal powers conferred by $P$; since $M$'s causal powers are literally included within $P$'s causal powers, the mental event does not compete with, exclude, or overdetermine the physical event.
- (B) A mental property is realized by a physical property if and only if the mental property has zero causal powers in the physical realm.
- (C) The subset account claims that physical properties are sub-components of spiritual consciousness, reversing the direction of physicalism.
- (D) Shoemaker argues that causal powers do not exist, and that causation is merely David Hume's constant conjunction of unpowered events.

**Correct Answer**: **(A)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
Sydney Shoemaker's *Physical Realization* (2007) represents one of the most prominent metaphysical solutions to Kim's exclusion problem:
1. **The Causal Theory of Properties**:
   - Shoemaker adopts a causal theory of properties: a property is individuated by the forward-looking and backward-looking causal powers it bestows on its possessor.
2. **The Subset Relation**:
   - Consider a mental property, such as *being in pain*. Pain confers a specific cluster of causal powers (e.g., disposition to wince, seek relief, withdraw limb).
   - The realizing physical property, $P$ (e.g., a specific complex neural firing pattern), confers all of those pain-related causal powers *plus* additional physical powers (e.g., releasing specific endorphins, heating up local capillary tissue).
   - Therefore, the causal powers of $M$ are a **proper subset** of the causal powers of $P$ ($	ext{Powers}(M) \subset 	ext{Powers}(P)$).
3. **Resolving Exclusion**:
   - Kim claimed that $M$ and $P$ are two distinct rival causes competing for the same effect.
   - But if $M$'s causal powers are a subset of $P$'s powers, $M$'s exercise of causal power is not a separate, rival causal contribution! A part does not compete with the whole of which it is a constituent.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(B) is incorrect**: If $M$ had zero causal powers, it would be an epiphenomenon, which Shoemaker's entire framework is designed to prevent.
- **(C) is incorrect**: Shoemaker is a committed physicalist; physical properties realize mental properties, not vice-versa.
- **(D) is incorrect**: Shoemaker is an ardent dispositional essentialist / causal realist about properties, explicitly rejecting Humean regularity theory.

**Primary Textual Reference**: Sydney Shoemaker, *Physical Realization* (Oxford University Press, 2007); Sydney Shoemaker, "Realization and Mental Causation", in *Physicalism and Mental Causation*, ed. Sven Walter and Heinz-Dieter Heckmann (Imprint Academic, 2003), pp. 321–333.

### Question 13: Ned Block on Phenomenal vs. Access Consciousness
**Format**: Conceptual Multiple-Choice (Format A)  
**Difficulty Tier**: Advance / Dialectical  
**Primary Thinkers**: Ned Block ("On a Confusion about a Function of Consciousness", 1995)

In his landmark paper *"On a Confusion about a Function of Consciousness"* (1995), Ned Block argued that philosophers and cognitive scientists routinely conflate two radically different concepts of consciousness: **Phenomenal Consciousness (P-consciousness)** and **Access Consciousness (A-consciousness)**. What is the definitive criterion of A-consciousness?

- (A) A state is A-conscious if its representational content is poised for direct use in reasoning, for the rational control of action, and for verbal report, whereas P-consciousness consists purely in the subjective, qualitative "what-it-is-likeness" of the experience.
- (B) A state is A-conscious if it can be directly photographed using an electron microscope.
- (C) A-consciousness refers to the unconscious reflexes of the spinal cord that bypass the cerebral cortex.
- (D) P-consciousness and A-consciousness are identical by definition, and no dissociation between them is logically or empirically possible.

**Correct Answer**: **(A)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
Ned Block's 1995 paper fundamentally restructured the cognitive science of consciousness:
1. **The Definitions**:
   - **P-Consciousness (Phenomenal)**: Experiential properties; the raw qualitative feel (qualia)—what it is like to see red, taste dark chocolate, or experience a migraine.
   - **A-Consciousness (Access)**: Functional and computational availability. A representational state is A-conscious if it is:
     1. Poised for free use in reasoning;
     2. Poised for the rational control of action;
     3. Poised for speech/verbal report.
2. **Dissociations**:
   - **A-consciousness without P-consciousness**: Philosophical zombies; super-blindsight (a patient who can spontaneously guess visual targets and act on them without any visual phenomenology).
   - **P-consciousness without A-consciousness**: Sperling's visual persistence experiments (subjects phenomenally see all 12 letters in a tachistoscopic array, but access capacity limits report to 4); the continuous ticking of a background clock that one phenomenally hears all afternoon but only accesses when it stops.
3. **The Philosophical Danger**:
   - Functionalist and computationalist theories of mind (e.g., Dennett, Baars) often claim to "explain consciousness" when they have merely explained access consciousness (information routing), leaving the Hard Problem of P-consciousness completely untouched.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(B) is incorrect**: Access consciousness is a functional/computational specification, not a microscopic physical object.
- **(C) is incorrect**: Spinal reflexes are non-conscious; they are neither A-conscious nor P-conscious.
- **(D) is incorrect**: Denies Block's central thesis, which argues that conflating P-consciousness and A-consciousness is a major error in philosophy of mind.

**Primary Textual Reference**: Ned Block, "On a Confusion about a Function of Consciousness", *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 18, no. 2 (1995): 227–247.

### Question 14: Christopher Peacocke on Non-Conceptual Perceptual Content
**Format**: Epistemological & Phenomenological Analysis (Format B)  
**Difficulty Tier**: Advance / Dialectical  
**Primary Thinkers**: Christopher Peacocke (*A Study of Concepts*, 1992; *Truly Understood*, 2008), John McDowell (*Mind and World*, 1994)

In the debate over the nature of perceptual experience, Christopher Peacocke argues that perception possesses **Non-Conceptual Content**, whereas Conceptualists like John McDowell (*Mind and World*, 1994) maintain that perceptual experience is conceptual "all the way down". What is Peacocke's **"Finely Grained Phenomenology" argument** in favor of non-conceptual content?

- (A) Human visual experience presents shades of color (e.g., hundreds of distinct shades of green in a lush forest) that vastly outstrip the finite stock of color concepts and linguistic demonstratives an agent possesses; therefore, the representational content of the experience cannot be constituted exclusively by the concepts the subject possesses.
- (B) Human perception is identical to digital photography, operating via discrete pixel coordinates that require zero conceptual interpretation.
- (C) Non-conceptual content refers exclusively to unconscious metabolic processes in the liver and spleen.
- (D) Concepts can only be acquired after an individual has learned the formal syntax of first-order predicate calculus.

**Correct Answer**: **(A)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
The debate between Conceptualism and Non-Conceptualism is central to contemporary epistemology and philosophy of perception:
1. **Peacocke's Non-Conceptualism**:
   - Peacocke (*A Study of Concepts*, 1992) argues that the content of perceptual experience is **non-conceptual**: a subject can be in a perceptual state with representational content without needing to possess the concepts required to specify that content.
   - **The Richness / Fine-Grained Argument**: Look at a paint swatch or a mountain vista. Your visual experience discriminates between *Red$_{29}$* and *Red$_{30}$*. You do not have distinct conceptual categories or linguistic words for each minute shade, yet your perceptual experience accurately represents the difference.
   - If perceptual experience were bounded by concept possession, your experience could only represent what your concepts classify.
2. **McDowell's Conceptualist Counter**:
   - John McDowell (*Mind and World*, 1994) countered that subjects possess demonstrative concepts (e.g., *"that specific shade of red"*), insisting that experience must be conceptual if it is to provide rational justification for empirical beliefs. Peacocke argues that demonstrative concepts presuppose the prior non-conceptual perceptual presentation.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(B) is incorrect**: Peacocke does not compare perception to raw digital camera pixels; non-conceptual content is still genuine *representational* content with accuracy conditions (scenario content).
- **(C) is incorrect**: Non-conceptual content is a feature of conscious perception, not internal visceral metabolism.
- **(D) is incorrect**: Peacocke does not place such absurd mathematical constraints on concept possession.

**Primary Textual Reference**: Christopher Peacocke, *A Study of Concepts* (MIT Press, 1992); John McDowell, *Mind and World* (Harvard University Press, 1994); Christopher Peacocke, "Does Perception Have a Nonconceptual Content?", *Journal of Philosophy* 98, no. 5 (2001): 239–264.

### Question 15: Hempel's Dilemma and the Definition of Physicalism
**Format**: Conceptual / Dialectical (Format C)  
**Difficulty Tier**: Advance / Dialectical  
**Primary Thinkers**: Carl Hempel ("Comments on Goodman's Ways of Worldmaking", 1980), Tim Crane and D.H. Mellor ("There is No Question of Physicalism", 1990)

**Hempel's Dilemma** poses a fundamental challenge to anyone attempting to define the thesis of **Physicalism** (the claim that everything is ultimately physical). Carl Hempel argued that physicalism is either trivially false or hopelessly vacuous depending on how "physics" is defined. What are the two horns of this dilemma?

- (A) If physicalism is defined in terms of **current physics**, it is almost certainly **false** (since current physics is admittedly incomplete and contains acknowledged anomalies); but if physicalism is defined in terms of an **ideal, future, complete physics**, it is **vacuous or empty** (since we have no idea what concepts an ideal physics will invoke, and it might even postulate mental or non-material entities to explain data).
- (B) If physicalism is true, then mathematics is false; if mathematics is true, then physics is false.
- (C) Physicalism can only be defined in English or German, making it culturally biased against ancient Sanskrit philosophies.
- (D) If particles exist, fields cannot exist; if fields exist, particles cannot exist.

**Correct Answer**: **(A)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
Carl Hempel formulated this dilemma in 1980, and it was powerfully developed by Tim Crane and D.H. Mellor in their 1990 paper *"There is No Question of Physicalism"*:
1. **The First Horn (Current Physics)**:
   - If physicalism asserts that the ontology of the world is exhausted by the entities and properties postulated by *current contemporary physics* (e.g., the Standard Model of particle physics and General Relativity), physicalism is almost certainly **false**.
   - Physicists themselves agree that current physics is incomplete: General Relativity and Quantum Mechanics are mathematically incompatible at Planck scales, and dark matter/energy remains unexplained. Future science will inevitably discard or revolutionize parts of current physics.
2. **The Second Horn (Future/Ideal Physics)**:
   - If physicalism instead asserts that the world is exhausted by what *ideal, fully completed future physics* will discover, the thesis is **uninformative and empty**.
   - We do not know what future physics will contain. If future physicists discover that conscious qualia or telepathic fields are required to unify cosmology, those would simply be baptized as "physical", making physicalism true by sheer definitional fiat.
3. **The Impact on Philosophy of Mind**:
   - Unless one can provide a principled "mark of the physical" (e.g., non-mentality, spatio-temporal location, mathematical tractability) that avoids both horns, the claim that "the mind is physical" lacks clear empirical and metaphysical content.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(B) is incorrect**: Hempel's dilemma concerns the definition of physics in physicalism, not the ontological status of platonist mathematics.
- **(C) is incorrect**: The dilemma is logical and metaphysical, completely unrelated to linguistic nationalism.
- **(D) is incorrect**: Conflates Hempel's dilemma with wave-particle duality in quantum mechanics.

**Primary Textual Reference**: Carl G. Hempel, "Comments on Goodman's Ways of Worldmaking", *Synthese* 45, no. 2 (1980): 193–199; Tim Crane and D. H. Mellor, "There is No Question of Physicalism", *Mind* 99, no. 394 (1990): 185–206.
