### Question 1: Jaegwon Kim's Causal Exclusion Problem for Non-Reductive Physicalism
**Format**: Conceptual Multiple-Choice (Format A)  
**Difficulty Tier**: Advance / Dialectical  
**Primary Thinkers**: Jaegwon Kim (1998, 2005)

In *Mind in a Physical World* (1998) and *Physicalism, or Something Near Enough* (2005), Jaegwon Kim formulates the **Causal Exclusion Argument** to demonstrate that Non-Reductive Physicalism collapses into either epiphenomenalism or full-blown reductive type physicalism. Consider the classic tetrad of physicalist commitments:
1. *Mind-Body Supervenience*: Mental properties supervene on physical properties.
2. *Mental Realism / Efficacy*: Mental events make a genuine causal difference in the physical world.
3. *Causal Closure of the Physical Domain*: If a physical event has a cause at time $t$, it has a complete physical cause at $t$.
4. *No Overdetermination*: Normal physical events are not systematically overdetermined by two independent, sufficient causes.

How does Kim demonstrate that these four principles force the Non-Reductive Physicalist to render the mental causally inert (Epiphenomenalism)?

- (A) Suppose a mental event $M$ causes another mental event $M^*$. By supervenience, $M^*$ must have a physical supervenience base $P^*$. To cause $M^*$, $M$ must cause its physical base $P^*$. But by Causal Closure, $P^*$ already has a complete, sufficient physical cause $P$. Unless we accept absurd, pervasive overdetermination, the physical cause $P$ completely excludes and preempts $M$, leaving $M$ with no independent causal work to do.
- (B) Because non-reductive physicalism denies that mental events have spatial mass, it violates Einstein's General Relativity equation $E=mc^2$.
- (C) Because supervenience is a symmetric relation, physical events must supervene on mental events, causing an infinite regress of causal attributions.
- (D) Because quantum indeterminacy at the synaptic cleft prevents brain states from obeying deterministic physical laws.

**Correct Answer**: **(A)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
Kim’s Causal Exclusion Argument is widely regarded as the most formidable internal challenge to non-reductive physicalism (such as Davidson's anomalous monism or functionalist token identity). 

The derivation proceeds as follows:
1. Suppose mental state $M$ (e.g., a desire to drink water) causes mental state $M^*$ (an intention to reach for the glass).
2. By the **Mind-Body Supervenience** thesis, mental properties cannot float freely; $M^*$ is instantiated at time $t$ because its physical base $P^*$ (a motor cortex activation) is instantiated. Thus, to cause $M^*$, $M$ must bring about $P^*$.
3. However, by the **Causal Closure of the Physical**, any physical event like $P^*$ has a *sufficient physical cause* at time $t-1$, which we call $P$ (the firing of premotor neurons).
4. Now consider $P^*$: it has a physical cause $P$, and a putative mental cause $M$. Are both causes operating? By the **Principle of No Overdetermination**, we cannot claim that every single voluntary action in human life is systematically overdetermined (like two bullets striking a heart at the exact same millisecond).
5. If $P$ is physically sufficient to cause $P^*$, what causal work is left for $M$? None. $P$ preempts and excludes $M$.
6. Therefore, either $M$ is strictly identical to $P$ (**Reductive Physicalism**), or $M$ is a causally impotent shadow cast by $P$ (**Epiphenomenalism**). The non-reductivist cannot have both mental distinctness and mental efficacy.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(B) is incorrect**: The exclusion argument does not rely on relativistic mass equations; it is an argument based on the completeness of physics, supervenience, and the rejection of systematic overdetermination.
- **(C) is incorrect**: Supervenience is explicitly an asymmetric or non-symmetric dependency relation ($B$ supervenes on $A$ does not mean $A$ supervenes on $B$); Kim never assumes mental-to-physical supervenience.
- **(D) is incorrect**: Appealing to quantum mechanics does not rescue non-reductive physicalism, because even in stochastic physics, probabilities are governed by physical wavefunctions, leaving no room for distinct non-physical mental intervention.

**Primary Textual Reference**: Jaegwon Kim, *Mind in a Physical World: An Essay on the Mind-Body Problem and Mental Causation* (MIT Press, 1998), Chapter 2: "The Causal Exclusion Problem", pp. 29–56.

### Question 2: David Chalmers's Two-Dimensional Semantics and the Zombie Argument
**Format**: Conceptual Multiple-Choice (Format A)  
**Difficulty Tier**: Advance / Dialectical  
**Primary Thinkers**: David J. Chalmers (1996, 2002)

In *The Conscious Mind* (1996), David Chalmers formulates the **Philosophical Zombie Argument** against physicalism:
1. It is conceivable that there is a creature physically and functionally identical to me, but lacking any conscious experience (*qualia*).
2. If it is conceivable that there is a physical duplicate of me without consciousness, it is **metaphysically possible** that there is such a creature.
3. If it is metaphysically possible that there is a physical duplicate without consciousness, then consciousness is not supervenient on the physical.
4. Therefore, physicalism is false.

Type-B physicalists (such as Brian Loar and Ned Block) respond by invoking Saul Kripke’s classic examples of the **necessary *a posteriori***: just as *"Water is H₂O"* is conceivable to be false (e.g., imagining water made of XYZ) yet metaphysically necessary, so *"Pain is C-fiber firing"* is conceivable to be false yet metaphysically necessary. How does Chalmers’s **Two-Dimensional Semantics** refute this Type-B physicalist response?

- (A) He demonstrates that while *"Water is H₂O"* involves a gap between primary intension (appearance) and secondary intension (underlying chemical essence), for phenomenal concepts like *pain*, **appearance is essence**—its primary intension and secondary intension coincide, meaning that primary conceivability directly entails secondary metaphysical possibility.
- (B) He argues that Kripke was mistaken about rigid designation, and that proper names are actually descriptive definite descriptions in all modal worlds.
- (C) He proves that water is not chemically identical to H₂O in modal counterfactuals containing heavy water ($D_2O$).
- (D) He shows that zombies are technologically realizable through advanced optogenetic CRISPR gene editing.

**Correct Answer**: **(A)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
Type-B Physicalism attempts to save physicalism by driving a wedge between **epistemic conceivability** and **metaphysical possibility**. They point to Kripke’s *Naming and Necessity*: Before modern chemistry, people could conceive of water not being H₂O (e.g., XYZ on Putnam's Twin Earth). Yet *"Water is H₂O"* is metaphysically necessary across all possible worlds. Similarly, Type-B physicalists claim: "We can *conceive* of a zombie, but that only reflects our conceptual limitations; metaphysically, any physical duplicate is necessarily conscious."

Chalmers deploys **Two-Dimensional Semantics** to dismantle this escape route:
Every expression has two distinct semantic dimensions (intensions):
1. *Primary Intension (Epistemic)*: Maps a world considered as *actual* to an extension. For 'water', the primary intension is "the watery stuff in our environment" (clear, drinkable, odorless liquid).
2. *Secondary Intension (Metaphysical)*: Maps a world considered as *counterfactual* to an extension, fixed by the actual world. For 'water', its secondary intension is H₂O.

In the case of "Water is XYZ", there is an epistemic/metaphysical gap *only because* the primary intension (watery stuff) diverges from the secondary intension (H₂O molecular structure). 

However, in the case of **phenomenal concepts** (qualia like *pain* or *redness*), there is no hidden microscopic essence beneath the surface appearance:
> "With phenomenal concepts, appearance is reality. The phenomenal feel of pain *is* the essence of pain. For phenomenal concepts, the primary and secondary intensions are identical."

Because the primary and secondary intensions coincide, the Kripkean gap between conceivability and possibility vanishes:
$$\text{Primary Conceivability of } (P \land \neg Q) \implies \text{Metaphysical Possibility of } (P \land \neg Q)$$
Therefore, since zombies are primarily conceivable, they are metaphysically possible, and physicalism is refuted.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(B) is incorrect**: Chalmers enthusiastically adopts Kripke's rigid designation framework; Two-Dimensional semantics is designed to formalize Kripke's modal insights, not reject them.
- **(C) is incorrect**: Heavy water ($D_2O$) is a peripheral chemical trivia that has nothing to do with the semantic distinction between primary and secondary intensions.
- **(D) is incorrect**: Philosophical zombies are not physically or biologically possible creatures (they violate physical laws); Chalmers’s argument is strictly about *metaphysical / logical possibility* ($P \land \neg Q$).

**Primary Textual Reference**: David J. Chalmers, *The Conscious Mind: In Search of a Fundamental Theory* (Oxford University Press, 1996), Chapter 4: "Naturalistic Dualism", pp. 131–171; and Chalmers, "Does Conceivability Entail Possibility?", in *Conceivability and Possibility*, ed. T. Gendler and J. Hawthorne (Oxford University Press, 2002), pp. 145–200.

### Question 3: The Extended Mind and Active Externalism
**Format**: Conceptual Multiple-Choice (Format A)  
**Difficulty Tier**: Advance / Dialectical  
**Primary Thinkers**: Andy Clark & David Chalmers (1998)

In their seminal 1998 paper "The Extended Mind", Andy Clark and David Chalmers formulate the thesis of **Active Externalism** using the famous contrast between **Inga** and **Otto**. Otto suffers from Alzheimer's disease and relies on a notebook where he writes down directions, addresses, and museum locations. What is the **Parity Principle** formulated by Clark and Chalmers, and what exact criteria must Otto's notebook meet to count as part of his cognitive apparatus?

- (A) The Parity Principle states: *"If, as we confront some task, a part of the world functions as a process which, were it done in the head, we would have no hesitation in recognizing as part of the cognitive process, then that part of the world is part of the cognitive process"*; and the notebook must be constantly accessible, automatically endorsed, and previously verified.
- (B) The Parity Principle states that human brains and electronic computers have equal ethical rights under international human rights law.
- (C) The Parity Principle claims that internal neural states are completely identical in spatial coordinates to the external objects they perceive.
- (D) The Parity Principle asserts that Otto’s notebook contains phenomenal qualia because cellulose fibers store subjective visual representations.

**Correct Answer**: **(A)**

#### Philosophical Grounding & Demonstration:
Clark and Chalmers distinguish their "Active Externalism" from Hilary Putnam's and Tyler Burge's traditional semantic externalism. Traditional externalism is passive and distal: what words mean depends on historical causal chains or social conventions (e.g., Twin Earth). Active externalism, by contrast, claims that **the physical machinery of mind itself extends beyond the skin and skull into the external environment**.

They illustrate this with the Inga/Otto thought experiment:
- *Inga* hears about an exhibition at the Museum of Modern Art on 53rd Street. She pauses, consults her biological memory, and walks to 53rd Street.
- *Otto* has Alzheimer's disease. He records all new information in a notebook. When he hears about the exhibition, he consults his notebook, finds the entry "MoMA is on 53rd Street," and walks there.

Clark and Chalmers introduce the **Parity Principle**:
> "If, as we confront some task, a part of the world functions as a process which, were it done in the head, we would have no hesitation in recognizing as part of the cognitive process, then that part of the world is part of the cognitive process."

To prevent trivialization (e.g., claiming the entire Internet or telephone directory is part of my mind), Clark and Chalmers specify strict boundary conditions for Otto's notebook:
1. *Constancy*: The notebook is a constant, readily accessible part of Otto's life.
2. *Automatic Endorsement*: Any information retrieved from the notebook is automatically trusted, without external skeptical cross-examination (just as Inga trusts her biological memory).
3. *Past Conscious Endorsement*: The information was consciously endorsed and recorded by Otto himself.

Under these conditions, Otto's notebook functions identically to Inga's biological non-occurrent beliefs, proving that beliefs and cognitive processes can reside outside the biological brain.

#### Distractor Analysis:
- **(B) is incorrect**: The Extended Mind thesis is an ontological and cognitive thesis about the boundary of mind, not an ethical doctrine about legal human rights for computers.
- **(C) is incorrect**: Active externalism does not claim neural states have the same spatial coordinates as external objects; it claims the cognitive process *includes* the external loop as a coupled system.
- **(D) is incorrect**: Clark and Chalmers explicitly restrict the Extended Mind thesis to **functional, cognitive, and intentional states** (beliefs, memories, computational processing); they explicitly bracket phenomenal qualia, acknowledging that phenomenal consciousness may remain internal to the nervous system.

**Primary Textual Reference**: Andy Clark and David J. Chalmers, "The Extended Mind", *Analysis* 58(1) (1998): 7–19.
